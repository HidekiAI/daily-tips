Not part of the rotation. Part 9 found structure in a generator's output by looking at it. This part goes one step further: if the output of a generator, or of anything that counts, reveals the internal state, then the future is no longer secret. Some generators make this easy on purpose, because the output is the state. Others hide the state in a long recurrence and give it up after enough outputs.

# What happened

**Bug class:** an output that reveals the generator's state, so the next outputs can be computed (CWE-340, generation of predictable numbers or identifiers). The 2008 attack below used a serial-number counter, not a random generator, but it needed the same property: knowing the next value.

**The story, in plain words:**

In December 2008 a team of seven researchers announced that they had made a certificate authority certificate that every common web browser trusted, and that they did not need the authority's permission to make it. The write-up is called "MD5 considered harmful today".

The hole was a hash function, MD5, which some certificate authorities were still using to sign certificates, years after cryptographers had shown that two different messages could be built with the same MD5 hash. To exploit that, the team needed a real, signed certificate from an authority whose signature would also be valid on a second, forged certificate they had prepared. A signature covers a certificate's contents, including its serial number and validity period. The team built both certificates together so their hashes collided. For that, they had to know the exact serial number and validity period the authority would put into the real certificate before the authority had issued it.

They estimated that building the colliding pair would take about three days of computing. So they needed to predict the serial number three days in advance. They went through certificates from several authorities and found that most serial numbers "looked random and thus hard to predict", but RapidSSL and FreeSSL used sequential ones. In the researchers' words: "One can think of the sequential serial number as a remote counter. Each certificate we purchase from the CA reveals the current value of the counter and increments it by one."

They measured the counter. In a data set of 9,251 RapidSSL certificates, the authority typically issued between 800 and 1,000 certificates over a three-day weekend, with low variance. So the plan was: buy a certificate on Thursday night to read the counter, predict it would be a little under the reading plus 1,000 on Sunday night, then buy certificates during the last hours to push the counter up to the target, reaching it about 30 seconds before the moment, and hope no other customer's request arrived in that window. It worked. The team ended up in possession of a rogue certificate authority certificate trusted by common browsers. Their recommended stop-gap for authorities that could not drop MD5 at once was "randomizing the serial numbers of all newly issued certificates".

The quotes, from Sotirov, Stevens, Appelbaum, Lenstra, Molnar, Osvik and de Weger, "MD5 considered harmful today" (30 December 2008):

- "Most of the certificates in our data set had serial numbers that looked random and thus hard to predict, but we noticed that RapidSSL and FreeSSL were using sequential serial numbers."
- "One can think of the sequential serial number as a remote counter. Each certificate we purchase from the CA reveals the current value of the counter and increments it by one."
- "As a proof of concept we executed a practical attack scenario and successfully created a rogue Certification Authority (CA) certificate trusted by all common web browsers."
- "As a last resort, in the case that MD5 cannot be discontinued immediately, a CA can take simple alternative countermeasures such as randomizing the serial numbers of all newly issued certificates."

The measurements below do the same to random generators: observe a few outputs, compute the rest.

## Rust: two consecutive 32-bit outputs of a 48-bit linear congruential generator leave exactly one candidate state of 65,536, and the next 1,000 outputs follow

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

The Java documentation for `java.util.Random` says it "uses a 48-bit seed, which is modified using a linear congruential formula", with each step `seed = (seed * 0x5DEECE66DL + 0xBL) & ((1L << 48) - 1)` and each 32-bit output the top 32 bits of the seed. The output hides only the low 16 bits of the state. In Rust, given two consecutive outputs, trying all 65,536 values for the hidden bits left exactly 1 candidate, and that state predicted the next 1,000 outputs correctly. The documentation's own comment is that the class is not cryptographically secure.

**Show me**

```rust
// rustc 1.99.1, std only: a 48-bit linear congruential generator as specified for java.util.Random
const MULTIPLIER: u64 = 0x5DEECE66D;
const INCREMENT: u64 = 0xB;
const MASK_48: u64 = (1u64 << 48) - 1;

fn fn_next_state(state: u64) -> u64 {
    (state.wrapping_mul(MULTIPLIER).wrapping_add(INCREMENT)) & MASK_48
}
fn fn_output_32(state: u64) -> i32 {
    (state >> 16) as u32 as i32 // the top 32 of the 48 bits
}

fn main() {
    let secret_state: u64 = (0x1234_5678_9ABC ^ MULTIPLIER) & MASK_48; // as if the seed had been scrambled
    let first_state: u64 = fn_next_state(secret_state);
    let second_state: u64 = fn_next_state(first_state);
    let (observed_first, observed_second): (i32, i32) = (fn_output_32(first_state), fn_output_32(second_state));

    let high_bits: u64 = (observed_first as u32 as u64) << 16; // the output hides only the low 16 bits
    let candidates: Vec<u64> = (0..65536u64)
        .map(|low_bits| high_bits | low_bits)
        .filter(|candidate| fn_output_32(fn_next_state(*candidate)) == observed_second)
        .collect();
    println!("candidate states after two outputs: {} of 65536 tried", candidates.len());

    let mut mut_state: u64 = fn_next_state(candidates[0]); // the state that produced the second observed output
    let mut mut_actual: u64 = second_state;
    let matches: u32 = (0..1000).filter(|_| {
        mut_state = fn_next_state(mut_state);
        mut_actual = fn_next_state(mut_actual);
        fn_output_32(mut_state) == fn_output_32(mut_actual)
    }).count() as u32;
    println!("the next 1000 outputs predicted: {} matched", matches);
}
```

Output: `candidate states after two outputs: 1 of 65536 tried` and `the next 1000 outputs predicted: 1000 matched`.

**Why it matters**

The pattern is "output = most of the state". Whenever a generator's output is a truncation of its state rather than a hash of it, a handful of outputs pin the state down, and brute force over the missing bits is trivial. Any Rust code that implements this family (a `u64` that is multiplied and added, with the high bits returned) has the same property.

**Chew on this**

Which generators in your code return a slice of their state? If someone saw two consecutive values, how many candidates would be left?

**Sources**

- https://docs.oracle.com/en/java/javase/21/docs/api/java.base/java/util/Random.html (48-bit seed, the formula, "not cryptographically secure")
- https://cwe.mitre.org/data/definitions/340.html

**Verified**

rustc 1.99.1, release build. The generator is the algorithm the Java documentation specifies, written in Rust; I did not run Java. The seed scrambling `^ 0x5DEECE66D` follows the documentation for `setSeed`.

**Gotchas**

- Two outputs gave exactly one candidate here; other states can give more than one before a third output removes the ambiguity.
- The probe does not use the `rand` crate: Rust's standard library has no random generator.

## C++: 624 consecutive `std::mt19937` outputs reveal its whole state, and the next 1,000,000 outputs were predicted without one miss, on GCC 16.2 and libc++

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

The tempering of a Mersenne Twister output is a fixed bijection of the state word, and each of the four steps can be undone. Undo the tempering on 624 consecutive outputs and you have the generator's entire 19,937-bit state. A program seeded from `std::random_device` observed 624 outputs, rebuilt the state, ran the twist recurrence itself, and then matched all 1,000,000 of the victim's next outputs, on GCC 16.2 and on clang 19.1.7 with libc++. The seed was never looked at.

**Show me**

```cpp
// g++ -std=c++23 -O2 untemper.cpp   (and clang++ -std=c++23 -stdlib=libc++ -O2 untemper.cpp)
#include <cstdint>
#include <iostream>
#include <random>
#include <vector>

constexpr int SHIFT_U = 11, SHIFT_S = 7, SHIFT_T = 15, SHIFT_L = 18; // the four tempering shifts of mt19937
constexpr std::uint32_t MASK_B = 0x9D2C5680u, MASK_C = 0xEFC60000u, MASK_ALL = 0xFFFFFFFFu;

// undo y ^= (y >> shift) & mask, and y ^= (y << shift) & mask: repeat the xor until the bits settle
std::uint32_t fn_undo_right(std::uint32_t tempered, int shift, std::uint32_t mask) {
    std::uint32_t partially_undone = tempered;
    for (int round = 0; round < 32 / shift + 1; ++round) partially_undone = tempered ^ ((partially_undone >> shift) & mask);
    return partially_undone;
}
std::uint32_t fn_undo_left(std::uint32_t tempered, int shift, std::uint32_t mask) {
    std::uint32_t partially_undone = tempered;
    for (int round = 0; round < 32 / shift + 1; ++round) partially_undone = tempered ^ ((partially_undone << shift) & mask);
    return partially_undone;
}
std::uint32_t fn_untemper(std::uint32_t output) {
    std::uint32_t word = fn_undo_right(output, SHIFT_L, MASK_ALL);
    word = fn_undo_left(word, SHIFT_T, MASK_C);
    word = fn_undo_left(word, SHIFT_S, MASK_B);
    return fn_undo_right(word, SHIFT_U, MASK_ALL);
}
std::uint32_t fn_temper(std::uint32_t word) {
    word ^= word >> SHIFT_U;
    word ^= (word << SHIFT_S) & MASK_B;
    word ^= (word << SHIFT_T) & MASK_C;
    return word ^ (word >> SHIFT_L);
}

int main() {
    constexpr int STATE_SIZE = 624;
    constexpr int PREDICTED_COUNT = 1000000;
    std::mt19937 victim(std::random_device{}()); // seeded from the operating system; the seed is never used below
    std::vector<std::uint32_t> state(STATE_SIZE);
    for (std::uint32_t& word : state) word = fn_untemper(victim()); // 624 consecutive outputs reveal the whole state

    int matches = 0;
    for (int predicted_index = 0; predicted_index < PREDICTED_COUNT; ++predicted_index) {
        const int slot = predicted_index % STATE_SIZE;
        const std::uint32_t upper_bits = state[slot] & 0x80000000u;
        const std::uint32_t lower_bits = state[(slot + 1) % STATE_SIZE] & 0x7FFFFFFFu;
        const std::uint32_t twisted = (upper_bits | lower_bits) >> 1 ^ ((lower_bits & 1u) ? 0x9908B0DFu : 0u);
        state[slot] = state[(slot + 397) % STATE_SIZE] ^ twisted; // the next word of the sequence replaces the oldest
        if (fn_temper(state[slot]) == victim()) ++matches;
    }
    std::cout << "observed 624 outputs, then predicted " << PREDICTED_COUNT << " more: " << matches << " matched\n";
}
```

Output (both libraries): `observed 624 outputs, then predicted 1000000 more: 1000000 matched`.

**Why it matters**

`mt19937` is the default engine people pick in C++, and 624 outputs is a short log. Shuffled cards, generated ids, a "random" delay: anything whose outputs an observer can collect and whose later outputs matter is predictable. The Mersenne Twister's quality for simulation is not in question; its role as a source of secrets is.

**Chew on this**

Does your service ever publish 624 consecutive outputs of one generator, for example as ids in a list endpoint? Who could read them?

**Sources**

- https://eel.is/c++draft/rand.predef (the `mt19937` parameters)
- https://cwe.mitre.org/data/definitions/340.html

**Verified**

GCC 16.2.0 and clang 19.1.7 with libc++, `-O2`. The tempering constants are those of the standard's `mt19937`; the exact match of 1,000,000 predictions is the check that they and my twist are right.

**Gotchas**

- The probe predicts forward from the 624th output; it does not recover earlier outputs.
- `std::random_device` seeds the victim with 32 bits (part 4); this does not matter, because the attack does not use the seed.

## F#: .NET's seeded `Random` is a subtractive generator whose `Next()` returns its raw words, so 55 outputs predicted the next 100,000 exactly

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Part 1 showed that `Random(42)` still runs the legacy algorithm. That algorithm keeps 55 words of state and updates one per call as `x[k] = x[k-55] - x[k-34]`, fixing the result up modulo 2^31 - 1. On .NET 10 the relation held at all 99,945 positions I tested for lag 34 and at none for lags 21, 24 or 31, and `Next()` with no argument appears to return the state word itself (my reading of the match, not of the source). An attacker who sees 55 consecutive outputs of a fresh `Random(12345)` computed the next 100,000 `Next()` values and matched all 100,000.

**Show me**

```fsharp
// .NET 10.0.14, dotnet fsi --optimize+
open System

// System.Random(seed) is a subtractive generator: Next() hands out its internal words directly.
// Find the offsets: does output(k) = output(k - 55) - output(k - lag) (mod 2^31 - 1) for some lag?
let victim: Random = Random(42)
let outputs: int[] = Array.init 100000 (fun _ -> victim.Next())
let modulus: int = Int32.MaxValue
let fn_fixup (raw: int) : int = if raw = modulus then raw - 1 elif raw < 0 then raw + modulus else raw
for lag in [ 21; 24; 31; 34 ] do
    let hits: int = [ 55 .. outputs.Length - 1 ] |> List.filter (fun index -> fn_fixup (outputs.[index - 55] - outputs.[index - lag]) = outputs.[index]) |> List.length
    printfn "lag %d: output(k) = output(k-55) - output(k-%d) holds for %d of %d positions" lag lag hits (outputs.Length - 55)

// attacker: sees only 55 outputs of a fresh instance, predicts the next 100000
let fresh: Random = Random(12345)
let seen: int[] = Array.init 55 (fun _ -> fresh.Next())
let predicted: ResizeArray<int> = ResizeArray<int>(seen)
for _ in 1 .. 100000 do
    let index: int = predicted.Count
    predicted.Add(fn_fixup (predicted.[index - 55] - predicted.[index - 34]))
let actual_later: int[] = Array.init 100000 (fun _ -> fresh.Next())
let matches: int = Seq.zip (Seq.skip 55 predicted) actual_later |> Seq.filter (fun (guess, actual) -> guess = actual) |> Seq.length
printfn "after seeing 55 outputs, predicted the next 100000 Next() values: %d matched" matches
```

Output: `lag 21 ... holds for 0 of 99945 positions`, the same for lags 24 and 31, `lag 34 ... holds for 99945 of 99945 positions`, and `after seeing 55 outputs, predicted the next 100000 Next() values: 100000 matched`.

**Why it matters**

The 21 in `_inextp = 21` from part 1 is what makes the lag 34: the generator reads the word 55 - 21 = 34 positions behind. A generator with 55 words of state is a generator that 55 outputs undo. `RandomNumberGenerator` is the documented API for secrets; the seeded `Random` is not one, and its `Next()` is the worst case, since the output is the state.

**Chew on this**

Where is a `Random(seed)` instance's output visible to a user: ids, tokens, shuffled orders? Would 55 of them be enough to predict the 56th?

**Sources**

- https://github.com/dotnet/runtime/blob/main/src/libraries/System.Private.CoreLib/src/System/Random.CompatImpl.cs
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.random

**Verified**

.NET SDK 10.0, `dotnet fsi --optimize+`. The fix-up rule (a result of exactly `Int32.MaxValue` becomes one less, a negative one gains `Int32.MaxValue`) is inferred from the measurement: the 100,000-of-100,000 match is the evidence. I did not read the implementation for this part; the lag-34 reading of `_inextp = 21` is my explanation.

**Gotchas**

- This is the seeded constructor's algorithm; an unseeded `Random()` uses a different generator (part 1) that I did not attack.
- `Next(max)` scales its output, so the same trick needs the unscaled `Next()` or more work.

## TypeScript: all 2^32 states of the popular `mulberry32` generator were tried in 4 seconds, one matched two observed outputs, and it predicted the third

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

`mulberry32` is a widely copied seeded generator for JavaScript with a 32-bit state, and the state advances by a constant on every call. On Node 26.13, a loop over all 4,294,967,296 possible states, comparing the first output with an observed value, finished in 4 seconds and left one state that also matched the second observed output. That state predicted the third output. A 32-bit state is a number a laptop can enumerate between sips of coffee.

**Show me**

```ts
// Node 26.13.0: save as mulberry.mts, run with node mulberry.mts
// mulberry32: a popular 32-bit JavaScript generator (the snippet is widely copied)
const const_fn_mulberry32 = (state_in: number): { output: number; state: number } => {
    const state: number = (state_in + 0x6D2B79F5) | 0
    let mixed: number = Math.imul(state ^ (state >>> 15), 1 | state)
    mixed = (mixed + Math.imul(mixed ^ (mixed >>> 7), 61 | mixed)) ^ mixed
    return { output: (mixed ^ (mixed >>> 14)) >>> 0, state }
}
const secret_seed: number = 0x1badf00d
const first_step = const_fn_mulberry32(secret_seed)
const second_step = const_fn_mulberry32(first_step.state)
const third_step = const_fn_mulberry32(second_step.state)

const started: number = performance.now()
const matching_states: number[] = []
for (let candidate: number = 0; candidate < 2 ** 32; candidate++) {
    if (const_fn_mulberry32(candidate | 0).output !== first_step.output) continue
    if (const_fn_mulberry32(const_fn_mulberry32(candidate | 0).state).output === second_step.output) matching_states.push(const_fn_mulberry32(candidate | 0).state)
}
console.log("states matching two observed outputs after trying all 2^32:", matching_states.length, "in", Math.round((performance.now() - started) / 1000), "seconds")
const recovered_third: number = const_fn_mulberry32(const_fn_mulberry32(matching_states[0]).state).output
console.log("third output predicted:", recovered_third === third_step.output)
```

Output: `states matching two observed outputs after trying all 2^32: 1 in 4 seconds` and `third output predicted: true` (the seconds depend on the machine).

**Why it matters**

State size is the budget of an attack: 2^32 steps is seconds, 2^48 (the Rust tip) is a smarter search that costs minutes, and 2^128 needs structure, as the C++ tip's 624 words show. A "seed" a user can pick, a clock value, or a 32-bit state all fall in the first category (part 4).

**Chew on this**

What is the state size of every seeded generator in your tests and your game code? Which of them is ever visible to a player?

**Sources**

- https://v8.dev/blog/math-random (V8's own statement that xorshift128+ "is still not cryptographically secure")
- https://github.com/v8/v8/blob/main/src/builtins/math.tq (Math.random's current implementation)

**Verified**

Node v26.13.0 in the `node:lts` image, one run. I tried to recover V8's `Math.random` state the same way and could not: the V8 source shows xorshift128+ returning the sum of two state words with the top 53 bits kept, but a Z3 model of that and several variants were unsatisfiable against Node 26.13's real outputs, while the same encoding was satisfiable on a synthetic stream. So I make no measured claim that `Math.random` is predictable here; the claim rests on V8's own statement. The reason my model did not match is unknown.

**Gotchas**

- The brute force relies on the first output to prune; with a slower output function it would take longer.
- `candidate | 0` turns the candidate into a signed 32-bit integer, which is what the generator's own arithmetic does.

# The functional angle: an invertible output function is an exposed state

Draws on all four tips. A generator is a pair of functions: a step `state -> state` and an output `state -> value`. If the output function is injective, the value is the state under another name. If it is "almost" injective (a truncation, a bijective tempering), a few outputs recover it. The measurements show where each of the four sits.

```rust
// Rust: the whole attack is one line: find states whose output equals the observation
let candidates: Vec<u64> = (0..65536u64).map(|low_bits| high_bits | low_bits).filter(|state| fn_output_32(*state) == observed).collect();
```

| Language | Output function | Outputs needed to know the state | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | top 32 of a 48-bit state | 2 | 1 candidate of 65,536 |
| C++ | invertible tempering of a 32-bit word | 624 | 1,000,000 of 1,000,000 predicted |
| F# | the state word itself | 55 | 100,000 of 100,000 predicted |
| TypeScript | a hash of a 32-bit state | 2 | 1 state of 2^32, 4 seconds |

F# is the baseline: the output function is the identity, which is the plainest version of the idea, and it is also the one .NET shipped for years. Which of your generators have an output function you could invert?

# The security angle: a log is a leak

Draws on all four tips and the 2008 attack. Mode B (what the language does not prevent). Bug classes: CWE-340 and CWE-338, with CWE-330.

```fsharp
// F#: do not hand a user consecutive outputs of a generator whose state they could rebuild
let ticket_random: Random = Random(12345)
let ticket_ids: int list = [ for _ in 1 .. 55 -> ticket_random.Next() ]  // each one reveals a state word of the generator
```

The 2008 team did not break a generator. They read a counter and pushed it. A sequential serial, a timestamp-based id, a 48-bit LCG and a Mersenne Twister are the same weakness at different sizes. The defense is the same: draw secrets from the operating system, and treat any value an outsider can see as a view onto the state.

| Language | What goes wrong | What the language does | Still on you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | an LCG's outputs reveal the state (measured) | the standard library has no generator | use `OsRng` or `rand::rng()` for secrets |
| C++ | 624 outputs reveal `mt19937` (measured) | no secure engine in the standard | the OS, or a vetted library |
| F# | `Random(seed).Next()` is the state (measured) | `RandomNumberGenerator` | do not expose seeded `Random` output |
| TypeScript | 32-bit generators fall in seconds (measured) | `crypto.getRandomValues` | never `Math.random` for secrets |

TypeScript is the baseline for the security reading because the weakest generator there takes only a few lines to write and a few seconds to break. If a stranger could fetch 55 of your ids in one request, what would they know?

# FAQ

**Did the 2008 team break MD5?**
They used a known collision technique for MD5 (their companion paper is cited on the page). The serial-number prediction was their own contribution on top of it: it let a collision built in advance match a certificate issued later.

**Is `mt19937` ever safe for security?**
Not as a source of secrets: the C++ measurement shows the state falls to 624 outputs. It is fine for simulation, where nobody is predicting it.

**Why did Math.random not fall in this part?**
I could not reproduce a recovery on Node 26.13, as the TypeScript tip's "Verified" paragraph explains. V8's blog says it is not cryptographically secure; I did not measure that.

# Appendix A: Minecraft worlds are 64-bit seeds that Java's 48-bit generator mostly ignores, and slime chunks give the rest away

Part 10's Rust tip recovers a 48-bit Java-style generator from two outputs. The same generator drives Minecraft's world, and a December 2021 student project report from Columbia University, "SeedCracker: A Parallel Minecraft Seed Reverse Engineering Tool" (Federick Gonzalez and Justin Chen, in Haskell), explains how far that goes in practice. The world is governed by a 64-bit world seed ("if the player does not manually enter in a seed, it is randomly generated"), but "Java Random uses a linear congruential generator (LCG) with a modulus of 2^48: seed(n+1) = (25214903917 * seed(n) + 11) mod 2^48. Thus, although it takes a 64-bit seed, only the lower 48 bits are used for most generation." Their tool attacks those 48 bits "independently of the other 16".

Their route is the game's slime chunks: the report's premise is that slime chunk positions, which depend on the world seed, can be read off a world, for instance from video of it, when the seed itself is lost. The report reduces the brute-force work in two steps. A 48-bit brute force "would take on the order of hours or days", but "when using Java Random's LCG, the lower 18 bits determine whether the final bit in the resulting number is 0 or 1, and therefore we can first reduce the search space by filtering the lower 18 bits". And because randomly generated seeds are themselves made by Java Random from two 32-bit integers, "the 48-bit seed actually corresponds to only a single world seed on average, and can be extended to the 64-bit seed nearly instantly".

The Rust tip's lesson in one more setting: a generator whose state is small, whose outputs leak a bit of structure each, and whose outputs a stranger can observe (here, in screenshots or video of a world) is a generator whose seed a stranger can find. I read the report's abstract, introduction and method sections; I did not run the tool.
