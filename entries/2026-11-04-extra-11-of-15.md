# Daily Tips special, part 11 of 15: random numbers, or the test a fair wheel fails once every twenty windows

<!-- special-nav -->
**Specials:** [<- previous: random numbers, part 10 of 15](2026-11-04-extra-10-of-15.md) | (last special)

Not part of the rotation. Parts 1 to 10 were about getting a random generator wrong. This part is about the people whose job is to check: the regulators and test laboratories that certify slot machines and roulette wheels, and the tests they require. Their rules are public, specific and short, and each one exists because of a way a generator can go wrong. Reading them is a compact course in what to test, and measuring them shows what the tests can and cannot tell you.

# What happened

**Bug class:** the generator was replaced by someone with access to it (CWE-506, embedded malicious code, for the allegation below), and the checks that would have caught a *weak* generator did not look for a *tampered* one.

**The story, in plain words:**

In December 2010 someone with their face hidden by a hooded top bought a lottery ticket at a gas station in Des Moines, Iowa. The Hot Lotto draw that followed was worth $14.3 million, and the ticket won.

Hot Lotto's numbers came from a random number generator on a computer system run by the Multi-State Lottery Association. The prosecution's case, as The Register reported it in July 2015, was that the association's information security director, Eddie Tipton, installed a hidden program, a rootkit, on the computer system so that he could secretly alter the generator and calculate which numbers would be drawn; that he also tampered with the security cameras covering the room; and that the winning ticket was bought by someone wearing a hoodie to hide their face. The association's IT director testified that Tipton had told him he had access to such a program. The software was never found, "because the company's hard drives had been wiped".

The point for a reader of this part is the shape of the failure. A lottery generator is audited for statistical quality: do the numbers look uniform? A generator controlled by someone who can compute its next output looks perfectly uniform. The audit measured the thing that was fine.

I read only a news report of the 2015 conviction. The search results I saw also mentioned later appeals and a later plea; I did not read them and make no claim about them.

The quotes, from The Register (22 July 2015):

- "Tipton, 52, installed a hidden rootkit on a computer system run by the Multi-State Lottery Association so he could secretly alter the lottery's random number generator, the court heard."
- "...although the software was never found, because the company's hard drives had been wiped."

The primary document for this part is the regulator's rulebook: the Nevada Gaming Control Board's "Technical Policies for Gaming Devices and Associated Equipment", notice 2012-51 of 24 May 2012. It says what a Nevada machine's generator must do. The tips below measure those rules.

## Rust: a perfectly fair 38-slot wheel fails Nevada's 95 percent chi-squared test in 4.88 percent of windows, and play is disabled after about 426 windows

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Nevada's policy for a hardware wheel says it must "determine if the RNG meets a 95 percent confidence limit using a chi-squared test for goodness of fit", on 10,000 outcomes, recalculated every 10,000 outcomes, and "disable play in the event that the wheel has failed to meet to the 95 percent confidence limit goodness of fit test for two consecutive testing periods". A test at 95 percent confidence fails a fair wheel five times in a hundred. In Rust on a simulated fair 38-slot wheel, 976 of 20,000 windows failed (4.88 percent), and the wheel was shut down by the two-in-a-row rule after 426 windows on average over 300 trials, about 4.3 million spins.

**Show me**

```rust
// rand 0.9.5, rustc 1.99.1, release build: the Nevada chi-squared rule applied to a perfectly fair 38-slot wheel
use rand::Rng;

const SLOT_COUNT: usize = 38; // a roulette wheel
const OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW: usize = 10_000; // from the policy: 10,000 outcomes per test
const CRITICAL_VALUE_95_PERCENT: f64 = 52.192; // chi-squared, 37 degrees of freedom, 95 percent

fn fn_window_fails(mut_rng: &mut impl Rng) -> bool {
    let mut mut_slot_counts: [u32; SLOT_COUNT] = [0; SLOT_COUNT];
    for _ in 0..OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW {
        mut_slot_counts[mut_rng.random_range(0..SLOT_COUNT)] += 1;
    }
    let expected_count: f64 = OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW as f64 / SLOT_COUNT as f64;
    let chi_squared: f64 = mut_slot_counts.iter().map(|count| (*count as f64 - expected_count).powi(2) / expected_count).sum();
    chi_squared > CRITICAL_VALUE_95_PERCENT
}

fn main() {
    let mut mut_rng = rand::rng();
    const WINDOW_COUNT: usize = 20_000;
    let failures: usize = (0..WINDOW_COUNT).filter(|_| fn_window_fails(&mut mut_rng)).count();
    println!("fair wheel: {} of {} windows fail the 95 percent test ({:.2} percent)", failures, WINDOW_COUNT, 100.0 * failures as f64 / WINDOW_COUNT as f64);

    // the policy disables play after two consecutive failed windows: how many windows does a fair wheel last?
    const TRIAL_COUNT: usize = 300;
    let total_windows: usize = (0..TRIAL_COUNT).map(|_| {
        let mut mut_windows: usize = 0;
        let mut mut_previous_failed: bool = false;
        loop {
            mut_windows += 1;
            let failed: bool = fn_window_fails(&mut mut_rng);
            if failed && mut_previous_failed { break mut_windows; }
            mut_previous_failed = failed;
        }
    }).sum();
    println!("a fair wheel was disabled after {:.0} windows on average (300 trials)", total_windows as f64 / TRIAL_COUNT as f64);
}
```

Output: `fair wheel: 976 of 20000 windows fail the 95 percent test (4.88 percent)` and `a fair wheel was disabled after 426 windows on average (300 trials)`. The numbers move by a little on each run (a second run: 972 windows, 4.86 percent, shutdown after 407 windows).

**Why it matters**

The rule is deliberate: a regulator would rather stop a fair wheel for an attendant to restart than let a bad one run. The cost is predictable, and the arithmetic says so: with a failure chance of 0.05 per window, two failures in a row take about (1 + 0.05) / 0.05^2 = 420 windows on average, and the measurement gave 426. The same arithmetic applies to any alert that fires on a test at a fixed confidence level: the alert rate of a healthy system is the significance level, not zero.

**Chew on this**

Which of your health checks or statistical alarms fires on a healthy system at a rate you could compute? Do you know it?

**Sources**

- https://gaming.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/gamingnvgov/content/about/industry-notices/2012-51.pdf (Regulation 14.040(2) policies)
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_test

**Verified**

rustc 1.99.1, rand 0.9.5, release build, one run each. The critical value 52.192 is the standard 95 percent point of the chi-squared distribution with 37 degrees of freedom, from memory of the table: the measured 4.88 percent failure rate agrees with it. The 420 is arithmetic.

**Gotchas**

- The policy applies this to hardware wheels; I applied it to a software wheel to measure the rule, not to claim that a software generator is subject to it.
- A real wheel has physical wear, which the simulated fair wheel does not.

## C++: the same chi-squared test catches a slot that is 50 percent too likely in 99.55 percent of windows, and one that is 10 percent too likely in only 9.35 percent

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

How good is a 10,000-outcome test at finding a biased wheel? On a 38-slot wheel simulated with `std::discrete_distribution` over `mt19937` on GCC 16.2, with slot 0 made more likely by a given percentage, the 95 percent test failed: 6.05 percent of windows at no bias (the false-alarm rate, 2000 windows), 7 percent at 5 percent extra weight, 9.35 percent at 10 percent, 29.75 percent at 20 percent and 99.55 percent at 50 percent. A bias big enough to matter to a player's expected return can sit below the test's ability to see it in any one window.

**Show me**

```cpp
// g++ -std=c++23 -O2 power.cpp
#include <array>
#include <iostream>
#include <random>

constexpr int SLOT_COUNT = 38;
constexpr int OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW = 10000;
constexpr double CRITICAL_VALUE_95_PERCENT = 52.192; // chi-squared, 37 degrees of freedom

int main() {
    std::mt19937 engine(2026);
    constexpr int WINDOW_COUNT = 2000;
    for (double extra_weight : {0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50}) {
        std::array<double, SLOT_COUNT> weights;
        weights.fill(1.0);
        weights[0] += extra_weight; // slot zero comes up a little more often
        std::discrete_distribution<int> biased_wheel(weights.begin(), weights.end());
        int detections = 0;
        for (int window = 0; window < WINDOW_COUNT; ++window) {
            std::array<int, SLOT_COUNT> counts{};
            for (int spin_index = 0; spin_index < OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW; ++spin_index) ++counts[biased_wheel(engine)];
            const double expected = static_cast<double>(OUTCOMES_PER_WINDOW) / SLOT_COUNT;
            double chi_squared = 0;
            for (int count : counts) chi_squared += (count - expected) * (count - expected) / expected;
            if (chi_squared > CRITICAL_VALUE_95_PERCENT) ++detections;
        }
        std::cout << "slot 0 is " << extra_weight * 100 << " percent more likely: test fails " << 100.0 * detections / WINDOW_COUNT << " percent of windows\n";
    }
}
```

Output (one run, GCC 16.2): `slot 0 is 0 percent more likely: test fails 6.05 percent of windows`, then 7, 9.35, 29.75 and 99.55 percent for 5, 10, 20 and 50 percent extra weight.

**Why it matters**

A statistical test has a detection curve, and a single window of 10,000 spins sits low on it for small biases. That is why the policy repeats the test every 10,000 outcomes and reports the last ten: it is a monitor, not a proof. And a tampered generator that produces uniform output is not on the curve at all: it fails no window, because the bias is in who knows the next number, not in the counts.

**Chew on this**

For the bias that would cost you money, what is the probability that your test, over one window, fails? How many windows would you wait to see it?

**Sources**

- https://gaming.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/gamingnvgov/content/about/industry-notices/2012-51.pdf
- https://eel.is/c++draft/rand.dist.samp.discrete

**Verified**

GCC 16.2.0, `-O2`, one run, 2000 windows per row. The 6.05 percent at zero bias is high by about two standard errors against 5 percent (standard error about 0.5 percent): a small excess I did not chase, so read the whole column as noisy to about plus or minus 1 point.

**Gotchas**

- The seed is fixed (2026) so the run is repeatable on one library; another library's `discrete_distribution` would give different numbers (part 1).
- 5 percent extra weight on one of 38 slots shifts that slot's probability from 2.63 to about 2.76 percent.

## F#: a wheel built from one random byte `% 38` fails the same test in 97.9 percent of windows, while `GetInt32(38)` fails 4.25 percent, a fair wheel's rate

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Part 2's modulo bias becomes visible to a regulator's test when the source is too narrow. A wheel built from one random byte `% 38` has 256 = 6 x 38 + 28, so 28 slots come up with probability 7/256 and 10 with 6/256. Fed through the 10,000-spin chi-squared test on .NET 10, 97.9 percent of 2,000 windows failed. A wheel built from 32 random bits `% 38` failed 4.65 percent, and `RandomNumberGenerator.GetInt32(38)` failed 4.25 percent: a fair wheel's rate. The 32-bit version has a bias too, of one part in 100 million, and the test cannot see it.

**Show me**

```fsharp
// .NET 10.0.14, dotnet fsi --optimize+
open System
open System.Security.Cryptography

let slot_count: int = 38
let outcomes_per_window: int = 10000
let critical_value_95_percent: float = 52.192 // chi-squared, 37 degrees of freedom
let window_count: int = 2000

let fn_failure_rate (label: string) (fn_spin: unit -> int) : unit =
    let fn_window_fails () : bool =
        let slot_counts: int[] = Array.zeroCreate slot_count
        for _ in 1 .. outcomes_per_window do
            let slot: int = fn_spin ()
            slot_counts.[slot] <- slot_counts.[slot] + 1
        let expected: float = float outcomes_per_window / float slot_count
        let chi_squared: float = slot_counts |> Array.sumBy (fun count -> (float count - expected) ** 2.0 / expected)
        chi_squared > critical_value_95_percent
    let failures: int = [ 1 .. window_count ] |> List.filter (fun _ -> fn_window_fails ()) |> List.length
    printfn "%-34s fails %.2f percent of %d windows" label (100.0 * float failures / float window_count) window_count

fn_failure_rate "one byte % 38 (256 = 6*38 + 28)" (fun () -> int (RandomNumberGenerator.GetBytes(1).[0]) % slot_count)
fn_failure_rate "32 bits % 38" (fun () -> int (BitConverter.ToUInt32(RandomNumberGenerator.GetBytes(4), 0) % uint32 slot_count))
fn_failure_rate "RandomNumberGenerator.GetInt32(38)" (fun () -> RandomNumberGenerator.GetInt32(slot_count))
```

Output (one run): `one byte % 38 (256 = 6*38 + 28)         fails 97.90 percent of 2000 windows`, then 4.65 and 4.25 percent (a second run: 97.90, 4.85 and 5.10).

**Why it matters**

The test is a useful tripwire for a narrow source and a blind one for a wide source with a tiny bias, which is where part 2's 2^-32 bias lives. The practical rule is the library's: use the function that rejects, and let the test catch the day someone replaces it with `% 38` on a byte.

**Chew on this**

If someone changed your wheel from `GetInt32(38)` to a byte modulo, which of your checks would fail first, and how long after the deploy?

**Sources**

- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.security.cryptography.randomnumbergenerator.getint32
- https://gaming.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/gamingnvgov/content/about/industry-notices/2012-51.pdf

**Verified**

.NET SDK 10.0 image (Ubuntu), `dotnet fsi --optimize+`, one run. The 1 in 100 million for 32 bits is arithmetic: 2^32 mod 38 divided by 2^32.

**Gotchas**

- Each spin asks the operating system for bytes, so the probe is slow; I did not time it.
- 4.25 and 4.65 percent are both inside the noise around 5 percent for 2,000 windows.

## TypeScript: Nevada requires a time seed to be in "milliseconds or smaller" and "not viewable"; Node's clocks step by 1 ms, 41 microseconds and 41 nanoseconds

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

The policy's seeding rule says a software generator must "Not use static seeds upon initialization", must be seeded with "a sufficiently unpredictable value", and that if a time or date is used "the value must both be represented in milliseconds or smaller, and must not be viewable anywhere on the gaming device". On Node 26.13 over two million consecutive readings, `Date.now()` advanced in steps of 1 ms, `performance.now()` in steps as small as 0.000041 ms (41 nanoseconds) and `process.hrtime.bigint()` in steps of 41 ns. All three meet "milliseconds or smaller". The second half of the rule, "not viewable", is the one a log line or an API response breaks.

**Show me**

```ts
// Node 26.13.0: save as clock.mts, run with node clock.mts
const const_fn_smallest_step = (fn_clock: () => number): number => {
    let smallest: number = Infinity
    let previous: number = fn_clock()
    for (let sample_index: number = 0; sample_index < 2_000_000; sample_index++) {
        const current: number = fn_clock()
        if (current > previous && current - previous < smallest) smallest = current - previous
        previous = current
    }
    return smallest
}
console.log("Date.now() smallest step (ms):", const_fn_smallest_step(() => Date.now()))
console.log("performance.now() smallest step (ms):", const_fn_smallest_step(() => performance.now()))
console.log("process.hrtime.bigint() smallest step (ns):", const_fn_smallest_step(() => Number(process.hrtime.bigint())))
```

Output (one run): `Date.now() smallest step (ms): 1`, `performance.now() smallest step (ms): 0.00004099999998175008`, `process.hrtime.bigint() smallest step (ns): 41`.

**Why it matters**

A finer clock is a larger seed space only if the attacker cannot narrow it. A start time that appears in a log, a response header or an uptime counter turns a nanosecond clock back into a few thousand candidates (part 4). The regulator's rule is shorter than the sentence it needs: seed from something unpredictable, and if you must use time, do not show it. The 41 ns step is a property of this machine's clock, not of Node.

**Chew on this**

Where does your process publish its start time: logs, metrics, an uptime endpoint? Does anything else derive from it?

**Sources**

- https://gaming.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/gamingnvgov/content/about/industry-notices/2012-51.pdf (Regulation 14.040(2): seeds and cycling)
- https://nodejs.org/api/perf_hooks.html#performancenow

**Verified**

Node v26.13.0 in the `node:lts` image (aarch64 container on Apple M3 Pro), one run, two million readings per clock.

**Gotchas**

- The "smallest step" is the smallest observed difference between consecutive readings; a clock can tick finer than any pair of calls can see.
- Browsers deliberately coarsen `performance.now()`; Node does not.

# The functional angle: a test is a function from outcomes to a verdict, and its error rates are part of its type

Draws on the Rust and C++ tips. `chi_squared_test: counts -> Pass | Fail` has a false-positive rate (the significance level) and a false-negative rate that depends on the bias. Writing the test as a pure function over counts makes both measurable by simulation, which is what the tips do.

```fsharp
// F#: the test as a pure function over counts, so its error rates can be measured by feeding it generated counts
let fn_chi_squared_fails (critical_value: float) (slot_counts: int[]) : bool =
    let expected: float = float (Array.sum slot_counts) / float slot_counts.Length
    (slot_counts |> Array.sumBy (fun count -> (float count - expected) ** 2.0 / expected)) > critical_value
printfn "%b" (fn_chi_squared_fails 52.192 (Array.create 38 263))
```

This sample ran and printed `false` (a perfectly flat wheel passes).

| Language | The test's measured behavior | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | 4.88 percent false alarms; shutdown after 426 windows | a healthy system trips its own alarm |
| C++ | 7 to 99.55 percent detection from 5 to 50 percent bias | a window of 10,000 sees large bias only |
| F# | 97.9 percent against a byte modulo | a narrow source is caught at once |
| TypeScript | clock steps 1 ms to 41 ns | the rule on seeds is about secrecy, not precision |

F# is the baseline because a pure counts-to-verdict function is its default shape. If your acceptance test for a generator is a single call, what is its false-alarm rate?

# The security angle: statistical quality is not secrecy

Draws on the Hot Lotto account and all four tips. Mode B (what the test does not prevent). Bug classes: CWE-506 and CWE-338, CWE-330.

```fsharp
// F#: this generator passes the chi-squared test and is a disaster: the output is a counter anyone can follow
let mutable mut_ticket_number: int = 0
let fn_next_slot () : int = mut_ticket_number <- mut_ticket_number + 1; mut_ticket_number % 38
```

A uniform output is a property of the counts. Unpredictability is a property of who knows the state. The Nevada rules carry both: the chi-squared test for the first, and the rules on seeds, background cycling and protecting pre-computed outcomes for the second. A lottery or a casino that checks only the counts has checked the part an insider never needs to break.

| Language | What the check proves | What it cannot prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | the output is flat enough (4.88 percent false alarms) | that nobody can compute the next one |
| C++ | large bias is absent (99.55 percent detection at 50 percent) | that small bias or tampering is absent |
| F# | the source is wide enough (byte caught, 32 bits passes) | that the seed is secret |
| TypeScript | the clock is fine enough | that the clock reading is hidden |

C++ is the baseline because its tip shows the test's limit most plainly. What would your audit say about a generator that fed the same counter to everyone?

# FAQ

**What does Nevada's policy require beyond the chi-squared test?**
From the notice: no static seeds, a seed that is "sufficiently unpredictable", cycling at 100 Hz when not in use, one game per RNG, separate RNGs for separate tables, and protection of any pre-determined outcome such as a shuffled deck. I read the notice, not the regulations it interprets.

**What does GLI test?**
GLI's own page for RNG certification lists source code review, the RNG period, its range, "the seeding/re-seeding", "the background cycling/activity", the DIEHARD battery of tests and outcome distribution tests. I did not obtain the GLI-11 standard itself.

**Was Hot Lotto's generator tampered with?**
That was the prosecution's case in 2015 as The Register reported it; I did not verify later proceedings.
