# Daily Tips special, part 14 of 15: random numbers, or the shuffle that was fair and the listeners who were right

<!-- special-nav -->
**Specials:** [<- previous: random numbers, part 13 of 15](2026-11-04-extra-13-of-15.md) | [next: random numbers, part 15 of 15 ->](2026-11-04-extra-15-of-15.md)

Not part of the rotation. Parts 1 to 13 were about randomness that was not random enough. This part is about the opposite: a generator that was exactly as random as promised, and users who found it wrong. They were right about something, and the something is a useful thing to measure.

# What happened

**Bug class:** none in the code. A mismatch between the specification ("uniformly random order") and what people mean by "random" (no obvious clusters). It is reported as a bug, fixed as a feature, and the measurements below show that the specification and the expectation are different programs.

**The story, in plain words:**

Spotify's shuffle button played songs in a random order. Some users complained that it played several songs by the same artist back to back, and asked why the shuffle was not random. Spotify's answer was that it was. In February 2014 an engineer wrote up what happened, in a post called "How to shuffle songs?".

"Since the Spotify service launched, we used Fisher-Yates shuffle to generate a perfectly random shuffling of a playlist," the post says. "However, perfectly random means that the following two orders are equally likely to occur": one with songs by the same artist spread out, one with them bunched. The team's first reaction was that the algorithm was beyond reproach. Their second, after they "read the comments more carefully", was that "some people don't want the same artist playing two or three times within a short time period".

The post names the mistake that users were making, and it is a famous one: the gambler's fallacy. If a coin has landed on Thai food four days running, people think Indian is due on Friday. It is not: "Throwing the coin for a millionth time is the same as throwing it for the first time." Hearing a song by one artist does not make the next song likelier to be a different artist in a perfectly random order. But the post's conclusion is not that users were wrong: "the old saying says that the user is always right, so we decided to look into ways of changing our shuffling algorithm so that the users are happier. We learned that they don't like perfect randomness."

So they changed it, borrowing from image dithering. Turning a grey picture into black and white pixels by random sampling gives "clusters and there are also big white spots"; Floyd-Steinberg dithering spreads them, and the clusters "almost fully disappeared". For a playlist, each artist's songs are stretched evenly over the whole list, with spacing that varies by about 20 percent either way and a random starting offset, and then the songs of an artist are shuffled among themselves. The algorithm "can be implemented in just a couple of lines", and it was rolled out to the desktop client first.

The quotes, from Lukas Polacek, "How to shuffle songs?" (Spotify Labs, 28 February 2014; I read the Internet Archive's 2020 copy, because the original address now returns an error):

- "We noticed some users complaining about our shuffling algorithm playing a few songs from the same artist right after each other."
- "Since the Spotify service launched, we used Fisher-Yates shuffle to generate a perfectly random shuffling of a playlist."
- "We learned that they don't like perfect randomness."
- "We can take inspiration from the dithering algorithms to solve our problem with clusters of songs by the same artist; we will try to spread them throughout the whole playlist."
- "We introduce a random offset in the beginning; otherwise all first songs would end up at position 0."

The measurements put numbers on the complaint, and show the clumpiness of true randomness in four small experiments.

## Rust: a uniform shuffle of 20 songs by 5 artists put some artist twice in a row in 96.2 percent of shuffles, and the spread shuffle in 0.3 percent

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Take a playlist of 5 artists with 4 songs each. A uniform Fisher-Yates shuffle (rand's `shuffle`) put at least one artist's songs next to each other in 96.2 percent of 200,000 shuffles. The complaint "I keep hearing the same artist twice" was not an illusion: it happens nearly every time. A dithering-style shuffle, with each artist's songs spaced along the list with a random offset and a spacing that varies by 20 percent either way, did so in 0.3 percent.

**Show me**

```rust
// rand 0.9.5, rustc 1.99.1, release build: a uniform shuffle against the dithering-style "spread" shuffle
use rand::seq::SliceRandom;
use rand::Rng;

const ARTIST_COUNT: usize = 5;
const SONGS_PER_ARTIST: usize = 4;

fn fn_playlist() -> Vec<usize> { (0..ARTIST_COUNT).flat_map(|artist| std::iter::repeat(artist).take(SONGS_PER_ARTIST)).collect() }

fn fn_has_adjacent_pair(playlist: &[usize]) -> bool { playlist.windows(2).any(|pair| pair[0] == pair[1]) }

// each artist's songs are spread along the playlist with a random offset and a spacing that varies by plus or minus 20 percent
fn fn_spread_shuffle(mut_rng: &mut impl Rng) -> Vec<usize> {
    let mut positioned: Vec<(f64, usize)> = Vec::new();
    for artist in 0..ARTIST_COUNT {
        let spacing: f64 = 1.0 / SONGS_PER_ARTIST as f64;
        let mut mut_position: f64 = mut_rng.random::<f64>() * spacing;
        for _ in 0..SONGS_PER_ARTIST {
            positioned.push((mut_position, artist));
            mut_position += spacing * mut_rng.random_range(0.8..1.2);
        }
    }
    positioned.sort_by(|left, right| left.0.partial_cmp(&right.0).unwrap());
    positioned.into_iter().map(|(_, artist)| artist).collect()
}

fn main() {
    let mut mut_rng = rand::rng();
    const TRIAL_COUNT: usize = 200_000;
    let uniform_with_adjacent: usize = (0..TRIAL_COUNT).filter(|_| {
        let mut mut_playlist: Vec<usize> = fn_playlist();
        mut_playlist.shuffle(&mut mut_rng);
        fn_has_adjacent_pair(&mut_playlist)
    }).count();
    let spread_with_adjacent: usize = (0..TRIAL_COUNT).filter(|_| fn_has_adjacent_pair(&fn_spread_shuffle(&mut mut_rng))).count();
    println!("uniform shuffle of {} songs by {} artists: some artist twice in a row in {:.1} percent of shuffles", ARTIST_COUNT * SONGS_PER_ARTIST, ARTIST_COUNT, 100.0 * uniform_with_adjacent as f64 / TRIAL_COUNT as f64);
    println!("spread shuffle: some artist twice in a row in {:.1} percent of shuffles", 100.0 * spread_with_adjacent as f64 / TRIAL_COUNT as f64);
}
```

Output (one run): `uniform shuffle of 20 songs by 5 artists: some artist twice in a row in 96.2 percent of shuffles` and `spread shuffle: some artist twice in a row in 0.3 percent of shuffles` (a second run: 96.1 and 0.3).

**Why it matters**

The uniform shuffle is the correct answer to the question the specification asked, and the wrong answer to the question the user asked. The second program is not a random permutation any more: some orders are impossible, and the algorithm is a distribution over a subset. Changing it is a product decision, and a measurement like this one turns the argument ("it is random") into a number the product team can read.

**Chew on this**

What does your user mean by "random" where your code means "uniform"? How would you measure the gap?

**Sources**

- https://web.archive.org/web/20200612074218/https://labs.spotify.com/2014/02/28/how-to-shuffle-songs/ (Polacek, read in full)
- https://docs.rs/rand/latest/rand/seq/trait.SliceRandom.html

**Verified**

rustc 1.99.1, rand 0.9.5, release build, 200,000 trials each, one run. My spread shuffle follows the post's description (random offset, spacing within 20 percent of even); it is not Spotify's code, and the post gives no code.

**Gotchas**

- The 0.3 percent is for 5 artists with 4 songs each; other mixes of artists give other numbers.
- A one-song artist (as in the post's picture) lands at a random place by the offset alone.

## C++: in 100 fair coin flips the longest run was 6.97 on average, and a run of 6 or more occurred in 80.6 percent of sequences

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Most people asked to write down a random sequence of flips avoid long runs, and real random sequences have them. On GCC 16.2 over 200,000 sequences of 100 flips from `mt19937` and `bernoulli_distribution(0.5)`, the mean longest run was 6.97, and the share of sequences containing a run of at least 5, 6 and 7 was 97.2, 80.6 and 54.0 percent. A sequence with no run longer than 4 is the unusual one.

**Show me**

```cpp
#include <algorithm>
#include <iostream>
#include <random>

int main() {
    std::mt19937 engine(14);
    std::bernoulli_distribution coin(0.5);
    constexpr int FLIPS = 100;
    constexpr int TRIALS = 200000;
    int at_least_five = 0, at_least_six = 0, at_least_seven = 0;
    double total_longest = 0;
    for (int trial = 0; trial < TRIALS; ++trial) {
        int longest_run = 1, current_run = 1;
        bool previous = coin(engine);
        for (int flip = 1; flip < FLIPS; ++flip) {
            const bool current = coin(engine);
            current_run = (current == previous) ? current_run + 1 : 1;
            longest_run = std::max(longest_run, current_run);
            previous = current;
        }
        total_longest += longest_run;
        at_least_five += longest_run >= 5;
        at_least_six += longest_run >= 6;
        at_least_seven += longest_run >= 7;
    }
    std::cout << "100 fair flips: mean longest run " << total_longest / TRIALS << "\n";
    std::cout << "a run of 5 or more occurs in " << 100.0 * at_least_five / TRIALS << " percent, 6 or more in " << 100.0 * at_least_six / TRIALS << " percent, 7 or more in " << 100.0 * at_least_seven / TRIALS << " percent of sequences\n";
}
```

Output: `100 fair flips: mean longest run 6.9707` and `a run of 5 or more occurs in 97.2235 percent, 6 or more in 80.5655 percent, 7 or more in 54.009 percent of sequences` (the digits change with the library).

**Why it matters**

If your test for "does this generator look random" rejects runs of 6 in 100, it rejects most true random sequences. If your simulation draws the "random delay" for a retry, three identical delays in a row are not a bug. The Spotify post's complaint, in coin form: a human sees a streak and concludes the source is biased; the source has no memory.

**Chew on this**

Which of your alarms treat a streak as evidence of a fault? What is the false-alarm rate for a healthy source (part 11's rule of thumb)?

**Sources**

- https://eel.is/c++draft/rand.dist.bern.bernoulli
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clustering_illusion (cited by the Spotify post as further reading)

**Verified**

GCC 16.2.0, `-O2`, one run, fixed seed 14. The 6.97 is a mean over my simulation, not a formula I checked.

**Gotchas**

- A different library's `bernoulli_distribution` would give different digits for the same seed (part 1).
- "Run" means consecutive equal outcomes in a row, longest in the 100.

## F#: 100 uniformly random points in 100 cells left 36.63 cells empty on average, and 0 of 20,000 trials filled every cell

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

Scatter 100 points uniformly over a 10 by 10 grid of cells. Each cell stays empty with probability 0.99 to the power 100, which is 36.6 percent, so about 37 cells are empty on average. On .NET 10, with `RandomNumberGenerator.GetInt32` choosing the cells, the mean over 20,000 trials was 36.63 empty cells (formula 36.60), at least 30 were empty in 99.0 percent of trials, and a layout with every cell occupied never occurred. A person asked to place 100 random points tends to spread them out, which is exactly what random does not do.

**Show me**

```fsharp
open System
open System.Security.Cryptography

let grid_size: int = 10
let point_count: int = 100
let trial_count: int = 20000

let fn_empty_cells () : int =
    let occupied: bool[] = Array.zeroCreate (grid_size * grid_size)
    for _ in 1 .. point_count do
        occupied.[RandomNumberGenerator.GetInt32(grid_size * grid_size)] <- true
    occupied |> Array.filter (fun cell -> not cell) |> Array.length
let empty_counts: int[] = Array.init trial_count (fun _ -> fn_empty_cells ())
printfn "100 uniformly random points in 100 cells: mean empty cells %.2f (expected %.2f), at least 30 empty in %.1f percent of trials"
    (Array.averageBy float empty_counts) (100.0 * (0.99 ** 100.0)) (100.0 * float (empty_counts |> Array.filter (fun count -> count >= 30) |> Array.length) / float trial_count)
printfn "a perfectly even layout would have 0 empty cells; random layouts had 0 empty cells in %d of %d trials" (empty_counts |> Array.filter (fun count -> count = 0) |> Array.length) trial_count
```

Output (one run; a second run gave 36.59): `100 uniformly random points in 100 cells: mean empty cells 36.63 (expected 36.60), at least 30 empty in 99.0 percent of trials` and `a perfectly even layout would have 0 empty cells; random layouts had 0 empty cells in 0 of 20000 trials`.

**Why it matters**

Random placement looks clumpy and has holes; "evenly spread but random-looking" is a different distribution, and the Spotify post's dithering is the way to get it. The same fact bites map generators ("why is there a big empty patch?"), load tests with random request times, and anything that picks independent random positions and expects coverage.

**Chew on this**

Where do you use independent random draws and expect them to cover a space? Would a low-discrepancy or stratified draw serve better?

**Sources**

- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clustering_illusion
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dither

**Verified**

.NET SDK 10.0 image (Ubuntu), `dotnet fsi --optimize+`, 20,000 trials, one run. The expected value is the formula `100 * 0.99^100`.

**Gotchas**

- 100 points in 100 cells is a convenient ratio, not a special one.
- Part 2 showed `GetInt32` as the unbiased way to pick a cell.

## TypeScript: after four or more heads in a row, the next flip was heads 50.01 percent of the time over 2.5 million such streaks

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

The gambler's fallacy is the belief that a streak makes the opposite outcome more likely. On Node 26.13, with 40 million flips from `crypto.getRandomValues`, there were 2,522,503 flips that followed a run of four or more heads, and 50.01 percent of them were heads. The coin has no memory; the Spotify post uses the same lunch-coin example.

**Show me**

```ts
// Node 26: does a streak change the next flip? Count heads after four heads in a row.
const const_flip_count: number = 40_000_000
const const_bytes: Uint8Array = new Uint8Array(65536)
let streak: number = 0
let after_streak_flips: number = 0
let after_streak_heads: number = 0
for (let chunk_index: number = 0; chunk_index < const_flip_count / (const_bytes.length * 8); chunk_index++) {
    crypto.getRandomValues(const_bytes)
    for (const byte_value of const_bytes) {
        for (let bit_index: number = 0; bit_index < 8; bit_index++) {
            const heads: boolean = ((byte_value >> bit_index) & 1) === 1
            if (streak >= 4) { after_streak_flips++; if (heads) after_streak_heads++ }
            streak = heads ? streak + 1 : 0
        }
    }
}
console.log(`after four heads in a row (${after_streak_flips} such flips), the next flip was heads ${(100 * after_streak_heads / after_streak_flips).toFixed(2)} percent of the time`)
```

Output (one run): `after four heads in a row (2522503 such flips), the next flip was heads 50.01 percent of the time` (a second run: 50.04).

**Why it matters**

The measurement is a sanity check on a source, in the opposite direction from part 11's: a generator that fails to show this property (a streak that makes the next outcome more or less likely) has memory it should not have. A user who expects it to, though, will call the correct generator broken.

**Chew on this**

Does your product ever promise "random" and mean "balanced over the short run"? Which of the two do you test?

**Sources**

- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambler%27s_fallacy
- https://nodejs.org/api/crypto.html#cryptogetrandomvaluestypedarray

**Verified**

Node v26.13.0 in the `node:lts` image, one run. The 50.01 percent has a sampling error of about 0.03 percentage points at this count (the standard error of a proportion), which I computed and did not measure separately.

**Gotchas**

- The probe counts every flip that follows a streak of at least four, so overlapping streaks are counted once per following flip.
- `getRandomValues` limits each call to 65,536 bytes; the probe loops.

# The functional angle: a specification is a distribution, and "random" is not one

Draws on the Rust tip and the Spotify post. A shuffle is a function from a playlist and a random source to a permutation. The uniform shuffle's distribution is uniform over all permutations. The spread shuffle's distribution is not: orders with adjacent same-artist songs have almost no probability. Both are pure functions given the random source, and the product question is which distribution to ship.

```fsharp
// F#: the spread shuffle's key idea as a pure function: each song gets a sort key from its artist's spacing, then sort by key
let fn_spread_keys (song_count: int) (offset: float) (jitters: float list) : float list =
    let spacing: float = 1.0 / float song_count
    jitters |> List.scan (fun position jitter -> position + spacing * jitter) (offset * spacing) |> List.take song_count
printfn "%A" (fn_spread_keys 4 0.5 [ 1.0; 1.0; 1.0; 1.0 ])
```

This sample ran. With no jitter and an offset of one half it prints `[0.125; 0.375; 0.625; 0.875]`.

| Language | The measured gap between uniform and "looks random" | Number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | uniform shuffle vs spread shuffle, adjacent same artist | 96.2 percent vs 0.3 percent |
| C++ | runs of 6 or more in 100 fair flips | 80.6 percent |
| F# | empty cells when 100 points fall in 100 cells | 36.63 |
| TypeScript | heads after four heads | 50.01 percent |

Rust is the baseline because it carries both distributions in a dozen lines. If you had to describe your "random" feature to a user as a distribution, would you say uniform?

# The security angle: people judge randomness by a standard attackers can exploit

Draws on all four tips. Mode B (what the human expects). Bug classes: none for the shuffle; CWE-330 for a generator someone "fixes" to look more random.

```rust
// Rust: do not use a "spread" generator where unpredictability matters; its output has structure by design
// a spread shuffle is a distribution over a subset of orders, so an observer can rule orders out
```

The sample is a comment, not code. The risk is in the fix: a team that responds to "it does not look random" by adding structure (no repeats, spread out) has made a less random generator, and for a lottery, a password, a token or a card deal the structure is information. For music, structure is the feature. The Spotify post is clear that it changed a shuffle, not a generator.

| Language | What goes wrong | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | structure added for appearance leaks which orders cannot occur | 0.3 percent adjacency |
| C++ | a test that rejects long runs rejects true randomness | 80.6 percent have a run of 6 |
| F# | a layout test that wants coverage rejects true randomness | 0 of 20,000 full |
| TypeScript | a source with "memory" is the broken one | 50.01 percent after streaks |

TypeScript is the baseline because its measurement is a property you want your generator to have. Would you rather your lottery looked random or was?

# FAQ

**Is Spotify's shuffle still this algorithm?**
I read the 2014 post; it says the algorithm was rolled out to the desktop client first. I did not check its current behavior.

**Did the post say users were wrong?**
It says they fell for the gambler's fallacy, and then that "the user is always right" and they should change the algorithm. Both are in the text.

**Why a distribution and not an algorithm?**
Because a uniform shuffle and a spread shuffle are both correct programs for different specifications, and the specification is the distribution.
