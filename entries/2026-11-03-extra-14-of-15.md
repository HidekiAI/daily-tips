Part 14 of the fifteen-part special on real incidents: a penny slot in Waterloo, Iowa, that displayed a $41,797,550.16 bonus in July 2011, as recorded by the Iowa Supreme Court in [McKee v. Isle of Capri Casinos, Inc.](https://cases.justia.com/iowa/supreme-court/2015-140802.pdf) (No. 14-0802, 2015). Each tip below is about a received number: a type wide enough to hold a value the sender could never legally send, a parse that turns a refund into a jackpot, and a validation attribute that crashes on the value it should reject.

# What happened

**Bug class:** not established. The test lab "was unable to definitively determine the exact cause", and the machine's maker suspected component degradation over time, which would make it an aging-related hardware fault rather than a code bug. The anti-pattern the tips below take from it is a receiver that accepts a value larger than its own channel can carry.

**The story, in plain words:**

On 2 July 2011 Pauline McKee, an 87-year-old grandmother of thirteen from Antioch, Illinois, was at a family reunion in Waterloo, Iowa. That evening her family gambled at the Isle Casino Hotel Waterloo. Around nine o'clock a daughter invited her to sit down at a penny slot machine called Miss Kitty. She had never played that game before.

Around 10:00 p.m. she wagered $0.25 on a spin. The screen showed a win of 185 credits, $1.85 under the game's rules, and beneath it a message: "Bonus Award - $41797550.16". The court notes it is undisputed that bonuses were not part of the Miss Kitty game.

She called an attendant. A supervisor photographed the display, a technician restarted the game, and the machine was blocked off. The supervisor paid her the $18.10 she had won up to that point, gave her a $10 card to play other games, and told her the casino would pay for her room. No employee ever told her she would receive the $41,797,550.16.

The Iowa Racing and Gaming Commission sent the machine's hardware and software to a testing lab, Gaming Laboratories Inc. (GLI). GLI found that the machine's logs showed it "thought it received a legacy bonus", a kind of bonus sent from the casino's central system. But the casino's system does not support legacy bonusing, and the command that sends one can carry at most $99999.99, "far less than was awarded by the game". GLI said it was "unable to definitively determine the exact cause", and that it was "highly likely" the message came from the machine's SPC 2.0 communication board.

The maker, Aristocrat, said it had known of such a possibility. In November 2010 it had sent the industry a technical bulletin about a "rare and unlikely circumstance", and said that "SPC2.0 component degradation over time may increase the susceptibility". The commission found the displayed amount "not valid", and the casino refused to pay.

McKee sued. On 24 April 2015 the Supreme Court of Iowa affirmed summary judgment for the casino: the rules of the game formed a contract, and under those rules she was not entitled to the bonus. The cause of the number was never established.

The point for the tips below: the game accepted a bonus far larger than the channel it came through could carry, and no check on the received number refused it. The court's own words follow.

From the opinion, quoting the Gaming Laboratories Inc. (GLI) analysis of the Miss Kitty machine:

```text
The logs on the machine do indicate that it thought it received a legacy bonus.
However, in reviewing the legacy bonusing aspect of SAS [the casino's "slot accounting
system"] it was noticed that the SAS legacy bonus command can send a bonus up to
$99999.99, which is far less than was awarded by the game. Furthermore, the system
does not support legacy bonusing.
```

The patron's spin paid "185 credits, or $1.85", while the screen said "Bonus Award - $41797550.16". The machine's maker, Aristocrat, wrote that "an erroneous bonus amount can be awarded to the machine" and that "SPC2.0 component degradation over time may increase the susceptibility to this rare occurrence." The Iowa Racing and Gaming Commission found the displayed amount "not valid", and the court held the casino to the game rules, which included "MALFUNCTION VOIDS ALL PAYS AND PLAYS." So the game accepted a bonus of $41,797,550.16 over a channel whose own command tops out at $99,999.99. The lesson I take from that, not a finding of the court or the lab: a receiver that checked the range of what it accepted would have refused it.

The number recurs. In August 2016 a Sphinx machine at Resorts World Casino in Queens printed a ticket for $42,949,672.76, as reported by [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2017/06/15/news/jackpot-steak-dinner-katrina-bookman-lawsuit/index.html) and [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/?p=348680); the New York State Gaming Commission called it a malfunction on a machine whose maximum was $6,500. No public document states the mechanism. The arithmetic is mine, not a source's: $42,949,672.76 is (2^32 - 20) cents, which is what minus 20 cents becomes when it is read as an unsigned 32-bit count. Treat that as a hypothesis the tips below can reproduce, not as the cause.[^nevada][^tribal]

[^nevada]: Nevada, court opinion and regulator. In [Sengel v. IGT](https://hallapproved.com/nv/cases/supreme/2000/2636967) (Nevada Supreme Court, 2000) a Reno machine stopped mid-spin when "the microswitch on the cash door of the bill validator reported ... that the cash door was open", with three jackpot symbols "in an uneven line"; the Gaming Control Board ruled it "not a valid win". The reverse also happens: in January 2022 the Board [secured a $229,368.52 jackpot](https://www.carsonnow.org/?p=31286) at Treasure Island that "a communications error on a progressive slot machine prevented the value of the jackpot from being known".

[^tribal]: Tribal casinos, court opinion. In [Dotson v. Tunica-Biloxi Gaming Commission](https://nill.narf.org/bulletins/federal/documents/dotson_v_tunica.html) (U.S. Court of Appeals, Fifth Circuit, 2020, unpublished) a machine "displayed "20 5," which he claims entitled him to a $20,500,000 jackpot"; the employees "could not find a "20 5" code in the slot machine manual", and the claim never reached the merits because the tribe had reserved its sovereign immunity.

## Rust: a `u32` field accepts the $41,797,550.16 bonus because it fits, and `#[serde(try_from)]` is how the protocol's own limit becomes part of the parse

*Type system deep cuts*

**Did you know**

4,179,755,016 cents fits in a `u32` (whose maximum is 4,294,967,295), so `serde_json` reads `{"bonus_cents": 4179755016}` into a `u32` field without a word: the type's range is not the channel's range. The container attribute `#[serde(try_from = "u64")]` makes serde deserialize a `u64` and then run your `TryFrom`, so an out-of-range bonus fails the whole parse with your message and the JSON position, and no message carrying it can exist. Measured on rustc 1.99.1 with serde 1 and serde_json 1.

**Show me**

```rust
// rustc 1.99.1, serde 1 (derive), serde_json 1. Debian, rust:1 image.
use serde::Deserialize;

const MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS: u64 = 9_999_999; // $99,999.99, the most a SAS legacy bonus command can send

#[derive(Debug, Deserialize)]
#[serde(try_from = "u64")] // deserialize a u64 first, then run TryFrom: an Err fails the whole parse
struct LegacyBonusCents(u64);

impl TryFrom<u64> for LegacyBonusCents {
    type Error = String;
    fn try_from(received_cents: u64) -> Result<Self, Self::Error> {
        if received_cents <= MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS { Ok(LegacyBonusCents(received_cents)) }
        else { Err(format!("bonus of {received_cents} cents exceeds the protocol maximum of {MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS}")) }
    }
}

#[derive(Deserialize)]
struct CheckedBonusMessage { bonus_cents: LegacyBonusCents }
#[derive(Deserialize)]
struct U32BonusMessage { bonus_cents: u32 }

fn main() {
    let miss_kitty_bonus_json = r#"{"bonus_cents": 4179755016}"#; // $41,797,550.16
    println!("{:?}", serde_json::from_str::<U32BonusMessage>(miss_kitty_bonus_json).map(|bonus_message| bonus_message.bonus_cents));
    // Ok(4179755016): the type is wide enough, so nothing complains
    println!("{:?}", serde_json::from_str::<CheckedBonusMessage>(miss_kitty_bonus_json).map(|bonus_message| bonus_message.bonus_cents.0));
    // Err(Error("bonus of 4179755016 cents exceeds the protocol maximum of 9999999", line: 1, column: 27))
    println!("{:?}", serde_json::from_str::<CheckedBonusMessage>(r#"{"bonus_cents": 185}"#).map(|bonus_message| bonus_message.bonus_cents.0));
    // Ok(185)
}
```

**Why it matters**

A server that decodes a message into a struct and validates it later has a window in which the invalid value is a perfectly typed field that any code can read. With `try_from` the check is the decode, so the value never exists. This is the same `TryFrom` that [part 11](2026-11-03-extra-11-of-15.md) used to turn a slice of 20 inputs into a fixed array of 21; here serde calls it for you, at the boundary where the bytes arrive.

**Chew on this**

Which fields in your own wire types are typed by their storage width (`u32`, `i64`) rather than by the range the protocol allows? If the sender could never legally send a value, does anything in your service notice when it arrives?

**Sources**

- https://serde.rs/container-attrs.html
- https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/convert/trait.TryFrom.html
- https://cases.justia.com/iowa/supreme-court/2015-140802.pdf

**Gotchas**

- A negative number never reaches your `TryFrom`: `{"bonus_cents": -20}` fails in serde's own `u64` step with "invalid value: integer `-20`, expected u64", and `1.85` fails with "invalid type: floating point `1.85`, expected u64". The error text you wrote only appears for values that are valid `u64`s.
- `-20_i32 as u32` is 4294967276, which is $42,949,672.76 when the unit is cents, and rustc gives no warning. Clippy's [`cast_sign_loss`](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust-clippy/blob/master/clippy_lints/src/casts/mod.rs) catches it, but the lint is in the pedantic group and "Allow by default": plain `cargo clippy` reported nothing, and `-W clippy::cast_sign_loss` said "casting `i32` to `u32` may lose the sign of the value". `u32::try_from(-20_i32)` returns `Err(TryFromIntError(NegOverflow))`.

## C++: `std::stoul("-20")` returns 18446744073709551596 with no exception, and narrowed to 32 bits it gives the $42,949,672.76 figure on the New York ticket

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

`std::stoul` calls `std::strtoul`, and `strtoul` accepts a leading minus sign: the digits are "negated as if by unary minus in the result type, which applies unsigned integer wraparound rules". So `std::stoul("-20")` returns 18446744073709551596, throws nothing and leaves `errno` at 0; cast to `std::uint32_t` it is 4294967276, the figure on the New York ticket if the unit is cents. `std::from_chars` (C++17) accepts a minus sign "only for signed integer types", so for an unsigned target it reports `std::errc::invalid_argument` and leaves the value untouched. Measured on Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2 in Docker; cppreference lists integer `from_chars` from GCC 8, Clang libc++ 7 and MSVC 19.14.

**Show me**

```cpp
// C++17. Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2 (Docker), -std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra: no warnings.
#include <cerrno>
#include <charconv>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <string>

int main() {
    const std::string const_refund_text = "-20"; // a 20-cent refund, arriving as text

    errno = 0;
    const unsigned long const_stoul_cents = std::stoul(const_refund_text); // strtoul negates in unsigned long
    std::printf("stoul: %lu errno=%d\n", const_stoul_cents, errno); // stoul: 18446744073709551596 errno=0

    const std::uint32_t const_ticket_cents = static_cast<std::uint32_t>(const_stoul_cents);
    std::printf("uint32_t: %u = $%u.%02u\n", const_ticket_cents, const_ticket_cents / 100, const_ticket_cents % 100);
    // uint32_t: 4294967276 = $42949672.76

    std::uint32_t from_chars_cents = 7; // mutable: from_chars writes through it, and leaves it alone on failure
    const std::from_chars_result const_parse_outcome =
        std::from_chars(const_refund_text.data(), const_refund_text.data() + const_refund_text.size(), from_chars_cents);
    std::printf("from_chars: invalid_argument=%d value=%u\n", const_parse_outcome.ec == std::errc::invalid_argument, from_chars_cents);
    // from_chars: invalid_argument=1 value=7
}
```

**Why it matters**

`stoul` is the parse people reach for because it throws: "std::invalid_argument if no conversion could be performed" and `std::out_of_range` on overflow. A minus sign is neither, so the one malformed input that matters for an amount sails through as a huge positive number. Rust's `u64` parse and serde both refuse "-20"; C++ gives you the refusal only through `from_chars`, which returns an error code you have to look at.

**Chew on this**

Where does your code turn text into an unsigned amount, size or count, and does that path use `stoul`, `strtoul` or `std::istream >> unsigned`? If a negative number arrived there today, which branch would it take?

**Sources**

- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/string/byte/strtoul
- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/string/basic_string/stoul
- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/utility/from_chars
- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/compiler_support/17

**Gotchas**

- `std::stoul(" 185abc")` is 185: leading whitespace is skipped and parsing stops at the first non-digit, without an error. Pass the `pos` argument and compare it with the length if trailing text should be refused.
- `std::stoul("-0")` is 0, so a test with "-0" cannot catch the negation.
- Assigning a negative `int` to a `std::uint32_t` is silent under `-Wall -Wextra` on both compilers. `-Wsign-conversion` warns on both: GCC says "may change the sign of the result", Debian clang says "implicit conversion changes signedness".
- `std::stoul` on a 20-digit number does throw `std::out_of_range` (message "stoul"): the check exists for too large, not for negative.

## F#: `[<Range(0, 9_999_999)>]` on an `int64` rejects $417,975.50 but throws `OverflowException` on $41,797,550.16

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

`System.Text.Json` ignores DataAnnotations entirely, so `JsonSerializer.Deserialize` reads 4179755016 into an `int64` field marked `[<Range(0, 9_999_999)>]`. The check only runs when you call `Validator.TryValidateObject`, and `RangeAttribute` has no `int64` overload: the `(int, int)` one converts the value to `Int32` first. 41,797,550 comes back as "The field bonus_cents must be between 0 and 9999999.", but 4,179,755,016 does not fit an `Int32`, so the validator throws `OverflowException` ("Value was either too large or too small for an Int32.") instead of reporting a failure. The bigger the bad value, the louder the failure, and not in the way you planned. Measured on F# 10 (SDK 10.0.403), FSharp.Core 10.1.0.0, .NET 10.0.14.

**Show me**

```fsharp
// F# 10, FSharp.Core 10.1.0.0, .NET 10.0.14 (dotnet fsi, sdk:10.0 image).
open System.Collections.Generic
open System.ComponentModel.DataAnnotations
open System.Text.Json

// RangeAttribute has (int, int) and (float, float) constructors, no int64 one.
type BonusMessage = { [<Range(0, 9_999_999)>] bonus_cents: int64 }

let fn_validation_outcome (bonus_message: BonusMessage) : string =
    let validation_results = List<ValidationResult>()
    try
        if Validator.TryValidateObject(bonus_message, ValidationContext(bonus_message), validation_results, true)
        then "valid"
        else validation_results[0].ErrorMessage
    with validation_failure -> validation_failure.GetType().Name

// JsonSerializer never looks at [<Range>]: both parse.
let over_max_message = JsonSerializer.Deserialize<BonusMessage> """{"bonus_cents": 41797550}"""     // $417,975.50
let miss_kitty_message = JsonSerializer.Deserialize<BonusMessage> """{"bonus_cents": 4179755016}""" // $41,797,550.16
printfn "%d %d" over_max_message.bonus_cents miss_kitty_message.bonus_cents // 41797550 4179755016

printfn "%s" (fn_validation_outcome over_max_message)   // The field bonus_cents must be between 0 and 9999999.
printfn "%s" (fn_validation_outcome miss_kitty_message) // OverflowException
```

**Why it matters**

An attribute on a record reads like a guarantee, but it is metadata that only one API consults, and that API fails differently on exactly the out-of-range values that matter most. In a web service the exception turns a "400, invalid bonus" into a 500, and whatever catches it upstream decides what happens to the request. F#'s own answer is a type whose constructor is the check (the functional angle below), which `System.Text.Json` cannot bypass because it never sees a public constructor.

**Chew on this**

Which of your request types carry validation attributes, and which code path actually runs the validator? If the validator throws instead of returning `false`, what does your error handling report to the caller?

**Sources**

- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.componentmodel.dataannotations.rangeattribute
- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.componentmodel.dataannotations.validator.tryvalidateobject

**Verified**

Run with `dotnet fsi` in `mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/sdk:10.0`, which is Ubuntu 24.04: .NET publishes no Debian SDK image.

**Gotchas**

- `[<Range(0L, 9_999_999L)>]` does not compile: FS0041, "No overloads match for method 'RangeAttribute'", listing only `(float, float)` and `(int, int)`.
- The `(float, float)` overload works for the large value: `[<Range(0.0, 9_999_999.0)>]` on the same field reported "must be between 0 and 9999999" for 4,179,755,016.
- The attribute lands on the generated property without a `property:` target: reflection found one `RangeAttribute` on `bonus_cents`, and the validator used it.
- An `int` field would not get that far: `JsonSerializer` throws `JsonException` ("could not be converted") for 4179755016, so the width check happens, just not the protocol's.

## TypeScript: `-20 >>> 0` is 4294967276, and `4179755016 | 0` is -115212280, so the usual integer coercions turn a refund into a jackpot and a jackpot into a debt

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

`>>> 0` is the common way to "make a number a uint32" in JavaScript, and it works on the operand's 32-bit two's-complement bits, so -20 becomes 4294967276: $42,949,672.76 if the unit is cents. `Uint32Array` stores the same 4294967276. Going the other way, `| 0` reads the low 32 bits as signed, so the Iowa bonus of 4179755016 cents becomes -115212280. The `number` type admits all of them, so the compiler has nothing to say. Measured on Node 26.13.0 with tsc 7.0.2 `--strict`.

**Show me**

```ts
// TypeScript, Node 26.13.0 (LTS), tsc 7.0.2 --strict.
const MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS = 9_999_999 // $99,999.99, the SAS legacy bonus limit

const const_refund_cents: number = -20
console.log(const_refund_cents >>> 0)                      // 4294967276
console.log(((const_refund_cents >>> 0) / 100).toFixed(2)) // 42949672.76: the New York ticket's figure
console.log(Uint32Array.of(const_refund_cents)[0])         // 4294967276: a typed array does the same

const const_received_cents: number = JSON.parse('{"bonus_cents": 4179755016}').bonus_cents
console.log(const_received_cents | 0)                      // -115212280

// What the protocol can carry, checked as a range: number says nothing about it.
const const_fn_is_legacy_bonus_cents = (candidate_cents: number): boolean =>
    Number.isSafeInteger(candidate_cents) && candidate_cents >= 0 && candidate_cents <= MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS
console.log([const_received_cents, 185, 1.85, const_refund_cents, Number.NaN].map(const_fn_is_legacy_bonus_cents))
// [ false, true, false, false, false ]
```

**Why it matters**

Bit coercions show up wherever JavaScript talks to a binary protocol: `DataView`, typed arrays, hashing, and any port of C code. Each one silently picks a 32-bit interpretation, so a value that crossed the boundary as the wrong sign or the wrong width comes out as a plausible, completely different amount. Unlike Rust's `as`, which the Rust tip shows at least has a lint, TypeScript has no type that says "fits in a uint32".

**Chew on this**

Which of your TypeScript paths use `>>> 0`, `| 0` or a typed array on a value that came from outside? If that value were negative, or above 2^31, what would the code after it do?

**Sources**

- https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Operators/Unsigned_right_shift
- https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Number/isSafeInteger
- https://money.cnn.com/2017/06/15/news/jackpot-steak-dinner-katrina-bookman-lawsuit/index.html

**Gotchas**

- `4179755016 >>> 0` is still 4179755016: the Iowa value fits in 32 unsigned bits, so the coercion that mangles -20 leaves it alone, and only a range check refuses it.
- `Number.isSafeInteger` is what refuses 1.85 and `NaN`; a plain `>= 0 && <= MAX` test passes 1.85.

# The functional angle: the channel's range is a parse that returns a result, run once where the bytes arrive

Draws on all four tips. The functional shape is `raw -> Result<LegacyBonusCents, reason>`, with the domain type constructible only through that function. The Rust tip already is this shape (`TryFrom`, called by serde); the other three are below, each run on 4179755016, 185 and -20.

```fsharp
// F# 10: a single-case union with a private constructor; the parse is the only way in.
module LegacyBonus =
    type LegacyBonusCents = private LegacyBonusCents of int64
    let MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS : int64 = 9_999_999L
    let fn_parse (received_cents: int64) : Result<LegacyBonusCents, string> =
        if received_cents >= 0L && received_cents <= MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS then Ok (LegacyBonusCents received_cents)
        else Error (sprintf "%d is outside 0..%d" received_cents MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS)
    let fn_cents (LegacyBonusCents bonus_cents) : int64 = bonus_cents

let received_cents_samples : int64 list = [4179755016L; 185L; -20L]
printfn "%A" (received_cents_samples |> List.map (LegacyBonus.fn_parse >> Result.map LegacyBonus.fn_cents))
// [Error "4179755016 is outside 0..9999999"; Ok 185L; Error "-20 is outside 0..9999999"]
```

```cpp
// C++23 (std::expected): Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2, -std=c++23 -Wall -Wextra: no warnings.
#include <charconv>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <expected>
#include <string_view>

constexpr std::uint64_t MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS = 9'999'999;
struct LegacyBonusCents { std::uint64_t cents; };

std::expected<LegacyBonusCents, std::string_view> parse_legacy_bonus(const std::string_view const_received_text) {
    std::uint64_t parsed_cents = 0; // mutable: from_chars writes through it
    const std::from_chars_result const_parse_outcome =
        std::from_chars(const_received_text.data(), const_received_text.data() + const_received_text.size(), parsed_cents);
    const bool const_is_whole_number = const_parse_outcome.ec == std::errc{} && const_parse_outcome.ptr == const_received_text.data() + const_received_text.size();
    return !const_is_whole_number ? std::unexpected(std::string_view{"not an unsigned number"})
         : parsed_cents > MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS ? std::unexpected(std::string_view{"above the protocol maximum"})
         : std::expected<LegacyBonusCents, std::string_view>{LegacyBonusCents{parsed_cents}};
}

int main() {
    for (const std::string_view const_received_text : {"4179755016", "185", "-20"}) {
        const std::expected<LegacyBonusCents, std::string_view> const_parsed_bonus = parse_legacy_bonus(const_received_text);
        std::printf("%s: %s %llu\n", const_received_text.data(), const_parsed_bonus ? "ok" : const_parsed_bonus.error().data(),
                    const_parsed_bonus ? static_cast<unsigned long long>(const_parsed_bonus->cents) : 0ULL);
    }
}
// 4179755016: above the protocol maximum 0
// 185: ok 185
// -20: not an unsigned number 0
```

```ts
// TypeScript, tsc 7.0.2 --strict: a branded number only the parse can mint.
declare const LEGACY_BONUS_BRAND: unique symbol
type LegacyBonusCents = number & { readonly [LEGACY_BONUS_BRAND]: true }
type LegacyBonusParse = { ok: true; bonus_cents: LegacyBonusCents } | { ok: false; reason: string }
const MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS = 9_999_999

const const_fn_parse_legacy_bonus = (received_cents: number): LegacyBonusParse =>
    !Number.isSafeInteger(received_cents) ? { ok: false, reason: "not an integer" }
    : received_cents < 0 || received_cents > MAX_LEGACY_BONUS_CENTS ? { ok: false, reason: "outside 0..9999999" }
    : { ok: true, bonus_cents: received_cents as LegacyBonusCents } // the one place the brand is minted
const const_received_cents_samples: number[] = [4179755016, 185, -20]
console.log(const_received_cents_samples.map(const_fn_parse_legacy_bonus))
// [ { ok: false, reason: 'outside 0..9999999' }, { ok: true, bonus_cents: 185 }, { ok: false, reason: 'outside 0..9999999' } ]
```

| Language | Functional form | Runs at decode time? | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F# | private single-case union + `Result` | no: `System.Text.Json` cannot build it, so you decode a raw record and parse it | one extra raw type per message |
| Rust | `TryFrom` + `#[serde(try_from)]` | yes: serde calls it | one `impl TryFrom` per field type |
| C++ | `std::expected` from `from_chars` | no: you call it on the text yourself | C++23 for `std::expected`; the text must be kept to parse |
| TypeScript | branded `number` + tagged union | no: `JSON.parse` returns `any`, the parse is a separate call | one `as` cast at the mint point, which nothing stops elsewhere |

F# is the baseline here because the private union constructor makes the parse the only way to get the type, with no cast anywhere; Rust is the only row where the decoder itself runs the check. If every amount your service received had to come out of a parse like this, which of your message types would turn out to have no maximum at all?

# The security angle: a range check that only tests the top lets a wrapped value through

Draws on the TypeScript and C++ tips. Runs in the direction where the language does not help: each of them can turn a valid-looking number into one of the opposite sign, and a check written against the expected sign misses it.

```ts
// TypeScript. Looks like a payout limit. The | 0 that "makes it an integer" has already made it negative.
const MACHINE_MAX_AWARD_CENTS = 650_000 // $6,500
const const_award_cents: number = 4179755016 | 0      // -115212280
console.log(const_award_cents <= MACHINE_MAX_AWARD_CENTS) // true: the limit check passes a negative award
```

```ts
// The check that holds: integer, non-negative, at most the maximum, on the value as received.
const const_fn_is_valid_award_cents = (candidate_cents: number): boolean =>
    Number.isSafeInteger(candidate_cents) && candidate_cents >= 0 && candidate_cents <= MACHINE_MAX_AWARD_CENTS
const const_iowa_bonus_cents: number = 4179755016
console.log(const_fn_is_valid_award_cents(const_iowa_bonus_cents), const_fn_is_valid_award_cents(const_award_cents)) // false false
```

The classes are [CWE-1284](https://cwe.mitre.org/data/definitions/1284.html), improper validation of specified quantity in input, and [CWE-195](https://cwe.mitre.org/data/definitions/195.html), signed to unsigned conversion error. The baseline for this failure is Rust, the one language here where the narrowing cast has a lint and the checked conversion is in the standard library.

| Language | What goes wrong | What the language does | Still on you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | `as u32` wraps -20 to 4294967276 | `u32::try_from` returns `Err(NegOverflow)`; Clippy `cast_sign_loss` (pedantic, off by default) | turn the lint on, and keep the protocol maximum in a `TryFrom` |
| C++ | `stoul("-20")` wraps, no exception | `from_chars` refuses the sign; `-Wsign-conversion` warns on the narrowing | use `from_chars`, check `ec` and `ptr`, then the range |
| F# | `[<Range>]` throws on an `int64` too big for `Int32` | the float overload of `Range` reports it | run the validator, catch its exceptions, or parse into a private union |
| TypeScript | `>>> 0` and `| 0` change the sign silently | nothing at the type level | `Number.isSafeInteger` plus both bounds, before any bit coercion |

Which of your limit checks test only the upper bound, on a value that has already passed through a cast, a shift or a parse you did not write?
