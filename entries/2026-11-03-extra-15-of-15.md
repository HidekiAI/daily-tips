# What happened

**Bug class:** not a code bug: a learned shortcut. The model found a feature that tracked the answer in the data it was given (which hospital, which scanner) and used it in place of the disease. The anti-pattern the tips below take from it is trusting a pattern that holds in your test data because of how the data was collected, not because of what it means.

**The story, in plain words:**

In 2018 a team led by John Zech asked a question that sounds too simple to need asking: does an AI that reads chest X-rays work as well in a new hospital as in the one it learned from? They had 158,323 X-rays from three hospital systems, the U.S. National Institutes of Health Clinical Center (NIH), Mount Sinai Hospital in New York, and Indiana University, and they trained a convolutional neural network, the kind of model that learns its own features from pixels, to spot pneumonia.

The model did well where it was trained and worse elsewhere: trained at NIH it scored 0.750 on NIH's own test images and 0.695 on Mount Sinai's (on this "AUC" scale, 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect). So the team asked what else the network could see. A second network, trained only to say which hospital an X-ray came from, got it right for 99.95% of NIH images and 99.98% of Mount Sinai's.

That mattered because the hospitals were nothing alike. Pneumonia was in 34.2% of Mount Sinai's X-rays and in 1.2% of NIH's. A "model" that looked at nothing but the hospital, and simply guessed "Mount Sinai means pneumonia", would already score 0.861 on the two hospitals together. The more a hospital predicted the disease, the authors wrote, "the more it was exploited to make predictions".

The clues were everywhere once someone looked. The network had learned the metal token that technicians place in the corner of the image to mark left from right. Inside Mount Sinai, the emergency department used a different maker's scanner and stored its images "in an inverted color scheme (i.e., air appears white)", and a network told those departments apart with 100% accuracy, while inpatients had pneumonia far more often (41.1%) than emergency patients (32.8%). "While these identifying features were prominent to the model," the authors wrote, "they only became apparent to us after manual image review."

Nothing in the code was wrong. The model did exactly what it was trained to do, on data where the hospital and the disease travelled together, and the internal test said it worked because the test data travelled the same way.

The point for the tips below: a pattern can pass every test you run on data from the same place, because the place is the pattern. The paper's own words follow.

From the paper's Discussion and Results:

```text
By engineering cohorts of varying prevalence, we demonstrated that the more predictive
a hospital system was of pneumonia, the more it was exploited to make predictions,
which led to poor generalization on external datasets.
```

```text
A CNN trained to identify hospital systems accurately identified 22,050 / 22,062
(99.95%, 95% CI 0.9991-0.9997) of NIH, 8,386 / 8,388 (99.98%, 95% CI 0.9991-1.0000)
of MSH, and 737 / 771 (95.59%, 95% CI 0.9389-0.9693) of IU test radiographs
```

The paper's Figure 2 caption: "we note that the CNN has learned to detect a metal token that radiology technicians place on the patient in the corner of the image field of view at the time they capture the image. When these strong features are correlated with disease prevalence, models can leverage them to indirectly predict disease." Its conclusion: "CNNs robustly identified hospital system and department within a hospital, which can have large differences in disease burden and may confound predictions."

## Rust: `HashMap` iteration order changes from one run to the next on purpose, so a test cannot learn it

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

Rust's `HashMap` documents that its algorithm "is randomly seeded" and that its iterators visit entries "in arbitrary order". In practice the order changes between runs of the same program: three runs of one binary printed three different orders for the same six keys. A test that happens to depend on the order fails some of the time, instead of passing forever on a coincidence. Swap in a fixed hasher and the order becomes the same on every run, which makes it learnable again. Measured on rustc 1.99.1.

**Show me**

```rust
// rustc 1.99.1, std only (Debian, rust:1 image).
use std::collections::hash_map::DefaultHasher;
use std::collections::{BTreeMap, HashMap};
use std::hash::BuildHasherDefault;

fn main() {
    let ward_names = ["icu", "er", "ward_3", "ward_10", "pediatrics", "oncology"];

    // RandomState: a fresh random seed per process, so the order changes from run to run.
    let random_order_wards: HashMap<&str, usize> =
        ward_names.iter().enumerate().map(|(ward_index, ward_name)| (*ward_name, ward_index)).collect();
    println!("{:?}", random_order_wards.keys().collect::<Vec<_>>());
    // run 1: ["oncology", "ward_3", "er", "icu", "ward_10", "pediatrics"]
    // run 2: ["icu", "ward_10", "ward_3", "er", "pediatrics", "oncology"]

    // A fixed hasher: the same order in every run, so a test can quietly come to rely on it.
    let fixed_order_wards: HashMap<&str, usize, BuildHasherDefault<DefaultHasher>> =
        ward_names.iter().enumerate().map(|(ward_index, ward_name)| (*ward_name, ward_index)).collect();
    println!("{:?}", fixed_order_wards.keys().collect::<Vec<_>>());
    // every run: ["ward_10", "er", "icu", "oncology", "pediatrics", "ward_3"]

    // BTreeMap: the order is the key order, and the type says so.
    let sorted_wards: BTreeMap<&str, usize> =
        ward_names.iter().enumerate().map(|(ward_index, ward_name)| (*ward_name, ward_index)).collect();
    println!("{:?}", sorted_wards.keys().collect::<Vec<_>>());
    // ["er", "icu", "oncology", "pediatrics", "ward_10", "ward_3"]
}
```

**Why it matters**

The Zech model passed its internal test because the test data shared the training data's accident. An iteration order is the same kind of accident: if it is stable, some test, snapshot or report will start depending on it, and the dependency surfaces only when the hasher, the data or the library changes. Rust's default removes the accident from your tests by making it change every run; C++, F# and JavaScript below all keep a stable order that is not a promise.

**Chew on this**

Which of your tests compare a map's output, a log line built from a map, or a JSON body serialized from a map, as text? Would they still pass if the order changed on every run?

**Sources**

- https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/collections/struct.HashMap.html
- https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/collections/struct.BTreeMap.html

**Gotchas**

- `BTreeMap` "produce[s] their items in key order", but string order is not number order: `"ward_10"` sorts before `"ward_3"`. A sorted map keyed on text with numbers inside has its own accidental order.
- The randomness is per map, not just per process: two `HashMap`s built from the same keys in the same run came out in different orders in 5 runs out of 5, so even two maps built side by side do not agree on an order.

## C++: a `std::set` of pointers iterates in creation order in a fresh process, until the allocator reuses a freed block

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

`std::set<const T*>` orders its elements with `std::less<const T*>`, which cppreference describes as "consistent with the implementation-defined strict total order over pointers": by address. In a fresh process, objects allocated one after another usually get rising addresses, so the set iterates in creation order and looks as if it means something. Delete two and allocate two more, and the new ones landed in the middle: `1 2 3 4 5 6` became `1 3 7 8 5 6`. Measured on Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2 in Docker, the same on both and on repeated runs.

**Show me**

```cpp
// C++20, Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2 (Docker), -std=c++20 -Wall -Wextra -O2: no warnings.
#include <cstdio>
#include <memory>
#include <set>
#include <string>
#include <vector>

struct Radiograph { std::string hospital_code; int study_number; };

int main() {
    std::vector<std::unique_ptr<Radiograph>> owned_radiographs;
    std::set<const Radiograph*> radiographs_by_address;  // ordered by std::less<const Radiograph*>: by address
    for (int study_number = 1; study_number <= 6; ++study_number) {  // mutable: loop counter
        owned_radiographs.push_back(std::make_unique<Radiograph>(Radiograph{"MSH", study_number}));
        radiographs_by_address.insert(owned_radiographs.back().get());
    }
    for (const Radiograph* const const_radiograph : radiographs_by_address) std::printf("%d ", const_radiograph->study_number);
    std::printf("\n");  // 1 2 3 4 5 6: looks like creation order

    // Free studies 2 and 4, then make two new ones: the allocator hands back freed blocks.
    radiographs_by_address.erase(owned_radiographs[1].get());
    radiographs_by_address.erase(owned_radiographs[3].get());
    owned_radiographs[1].reset();
    owned_radiographs[3].reset();
    for (int study_number = 7; study_number <= 8; ++study_number) {  // mutable: loop counter
        owned_radiographs.push_back(std::make_unique<Radiograph>(Radiograph{"MSH", study_number}));
        radiographs_by_address.insert(owned_radiographs.back().get());
    }
    for (const Radiograph* const const_radiograph : radiographs_by_address) std::printf("%d ", const_radiograph->study_number);
    std::printf("\n");  // 1 3 7 8 5 6: the "order" was the allocator's
}
```

**Why it matters**

A unit test builds its objects in a fresh process, in order, with nothing freed, which is exactly the condition under which address order equals creation order. A long-running server frees and reuses memory all day, so the same code iterates in an order that depends on its history. The test passed for the reason the Zech model's internal test passed: it ran on data collected the same way as the data it was tuned on.

**Chew on this**

Where does your C++ code keep a `std::set` or `std::map` keyed by pointer, and does anything downstream (output, a hash, a "first" element) depend on the order it iterates in?

**Sources**

- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/utility/functional/less
- https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/container/set

**Gotchas**

- `std::less` gives a total order over pointers, so the set is well-defined; it is the *meaning* of that order that is accidental. The order is correct, stable and reproducible on one machine, which is what makes a test trust it.
- Address space layout randomization moves the addresses from run to run but, in this probe, not their relative order: two runs printed the same sequences, so ASLR does not make the accident visible the way Rust's random seed does.

## F#: a `Dictionary` enumerates in insertion order until a `Remove`, and then the next `Add` takes the freed slot

*Stdlib and ecosystem gems*

**Did you know**

.NET's documentation for `Dictionary<TKey, TValue>` says: "The order in which the items are returned is undefined." In practice a dictionary that only ever grows enumerates in insertion order, so code and tests come to read it as an ordered list. Remove one key and add another, and the new key appears where the removed one was: after removing `"er"` and adding `"oncology"`, `"oncology"` came second, not last. F#'s own `Map` is sorted by key, and says so in its type. Measured on F# 10 (SDK 10.0.403), FSharp.Core 10.1.0.0, .NET 10.0.14.

**Show me**

```fsharp
// F# 10, FSharp.Core 10.1.0.0, .NET 10.0.14 (dotnet fsi, sdk:10.0 image).
open System.Collections.Generic

let wards_by_name = Dictionary<string, int>()
for ward_name, ward_index in [ "icu", 1; "er", 2; "ward_3", 3; "ward_10", 4; "pediatrics", 5 ] do
    wards_by_name.Add(ward_name, ward_index)
printfn "%A" (List.ofSeq wards_by_name.Keys)
// ["icu"; "er"; "ward_3"; "ward_10"; "pediatrics"]: looks like insertion order

wards_by_name.Remove "er" |> ignore
wards_by_name.Add("oncology", 6)
printfn "%A" (List.ofSeq wards_by_name.Keys)
// ["icu"; "oncology"; "ward_3"; "ward_10"; "pediatrics"]: the new key took the freed slot

let sorted_wards = Map.ofList [ "icu", 1; "er", 2; "ward_3", 3; "ward_10", 4; "pediatrics", 5 ]
printfn "%A" (sorted_wards |> Map.keys |> List.ofSeq)
// ["er"; "icu"; "pediatrics"; "ward_10"; "ward_3"]: sorted by key, by contract
```

**Why it matters**

Test fixtures are usually built by adding entries and never removing any, so the undefined order looks defined in every test. Production data is edited: wards close, sessions expire, entries are evicted, and the order quietly changes. Any report, export or "first match" built by enumerating a `Dictionary` is keyed on its edit history, a property no test fixture had.

**Chew on this**

Which of your F# or C# code enumerates a `Dictionary` and keeps the first, the last, or the order? Does any test of it ever remove a key before it checks the result?

**Sources**

- https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/system.collections.generic.dictionary-2
- https://fsharp.github.io/fsharp-core-docs/reference/fsharp-collections-fsharpmap-2.html

**Verified**

Run with `dotnet fsi` in `mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/sdk:10.0`, which is Ubuntu 24.04: .NET publishes no Debian SDK image.

**Gotchas**

- `Map` sorts by key with ordinal string comparison here, so `"ward_10"` comes before `"ward_3"`, as in Rust's `BTreeMap`: the sorted order is defined, but not necessarily the order a reader expects.
- The insertion-order behaviour is an implementation detail of today's `Dictionary`, which the documentation explicitly does not promise; `SortedDictionary` and `Map` are the types whose order is part of the contract.

## TypeScript: object keys keep insertion order except the integer-like ones, which jump to the front in numeric order

*Idioms and antipatterns*

**Did you know**

A plain object keeps string keys in the order they were added, which is why code reads it as ordered. But keys that look like array indexes (`"3"`, `"10"`, anything from `"0"` to `"4294967294"`) are listed first, in ascending numeric order, before every other key, as the ECMAScript specification's `OrdinaryOwnPropertyKeys` defines. Keys added as `icu`, `er`, `10`, `3`, `03` come back as `3, 10, icu, er, 03`: `"03"` is not index-like, so it stays put. `JSON.stringify` follows the same order, and a `Map` keeps true insertion order. Measured on Node 26.13.0 with tsc 7.0.2 `--strict`.

**Show me**

```ts
// TypeScript, Node 26.13.0 (LTS), tsc 7.0.2 --strict.
const const_ward_beds: Record<string, number> = {}
const_ward_beds["icu"] = 8
const_ward_beds["er"] = 12
const_ward_beds["10"] = 30
const_ward_beds["3"] = 24
const_ward_beds["03"] = 20
console.log(Object.keys(const_ward_beds))     // [ '3', '10', 'icu', 'er', '03' ]
console.log(JSON.stringify(const_ward_beds))  // {"3":24,"10":30,"icu":8,"er":12,"03":20}

const const_ward_beds_map = new Map<string, number>([["icu", 8], ["er", 12], ["10", 30], ["3", 24], ["03", 20]])
console.log([...const_ward_beds_map.keys()])  // [ 'icu', 'er', '10', '3', '03' ]: insertion order, always

const const_parsed_wards: Record<string, number> = JSON.parse('{"icu": 8, "er": 12, "10": 30}')
console.log(Object.keys(const_parsed_wards)[0])  // 10: the "first" ward is no longer the first one written
```

**Why it matters**

A test fixture with wards named `icu` and `er` shows insertion order, and the code that keeps "the first entry" or renders a table in key order passes. Real data arrives with numeric ward codes, room numbers or user IDs as keys, and those silently move to the front. The order the code relied on was a property of the fixture's names, which is the Zech failure in miniature.

**Chew on this**

Where does your TypeScript code treat an object, or `JSON.parse` output, as ordered: the first key, the column order of a table, a hash or signature over `JSON.stringify`? Could any of those keys ever be a number?

**Sources**

- https://tc39.es/ecma262/#sec-ordinaryownpropertykeys
- https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Object/keys

**Gotchas**

- The cut-off is the array-index range: `"4294967294"` moved to the front, while `"4294967295"`, `"-1"` and `"1.5"` kept their insertion order.
- `Record<string, number>` says nothing about order, and TypeScript has no type that does; the only ordered key-value type in the standard library is `Map`.

# The functional angle: make the order part of the value, or do not depend on it

Draws on all four tips. The functional fix is the same shape everywhere: when order matters, it is computed from the data by a stated key, not inherited from how a container happened to be built. Each row sorts the same five wards by bed count, highest first.

```rust
// Rust 1.99.1: the order comes from a key the code names.
let mut mut_ward_beds = vec![("icu", 8), ("er", 12), ("10", 30), ("3", 24), ("03", 20)]; // mutable: sorted in place
mut_ward_beds.sort_by_key(|(_, bed_count)| std::cmp::Reverse(*bed_count));
println!("{:?}", mut_ward_beds); // [("10", 30), ("3", 24), ("03", 20), ("er", 12), ("icu", 8)]
```

```fsharp
// F# 10: List.sortByDescending returns a new list; nothing is mutated.
[ "icu", 8; "er", 12; "10", 30; "3", 24; "03", 20 ]
|> List.sortByDescending snd
|> printfn "%A" // [("10", 30); ("3", 24); ("03", 20); ("er", 12); ("icu", 8)]
```

```cpp
// C++20 (Debian clang 19.1.7 and GCC 16.2): std::ranges::sort with a projection.
std::vector<std::pair<std::string, int>> ward_beds{{"icu", 8}, {"er", 12}, {"10", 30}, {"3", 24}, {"03", 20}}; // mutable: sorted in place
std::ranges::sort(ward_beds, std::ranges::greater{}, &std::pair<std::string, int>::second);
// 10 30, 3 24, 03 20, er 12, icu 8
```

```ts
// TypeScript (Node 26.13.0): toSorted returns a new array, from entries, not from key order.
const const_sorted_wards = Object.entries({ icu: 8, er: 12, "10": 30, "3": 24, "03": 20 }).toSorted(
    ([, left_bed_count], [, right_bed_count]) => right_bed_count - left_bed_count)
console.log(const_sorted_wards) // [ [ '10', 30 ], [ '3', 24 ], [ '03', 20 ], [ 'er', 12 ], [ 'icu', 8 ] ]
```

| Language | Functional form | Needs mutation? | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F# | `List.sortByDescending snd` | no | a new list |
| TypeScript | `toSorted` over `Object.entries` | no | a new array; the key order of the object no longer matters |
| Rust | `sort_by_key` with `Reverse` | yes, `let mut` (an in-place sort) | nothing extra; an iterator chain would need `collect` then sort |
| C++ | `std::ranges::sort` with a projection | yes, the vector is sorted in place | nothing extra |

F# is the baseline because its default sort is the non-mutating one; Rust and C++ sort in place, so the order becomes a state of the container again, and only the stated key keeps it honest. If every ordered output in your code had to name its sort key, which ones would turn out to have none?

# The security angle: whoever chooses the keys chooses the order

Draws on the TypeScript tip, with the others as control rows. Runs in the direction where the language does not help: a "first match wins" rule over an object lets anyone who can add a key decide what comes first.

```ts
// TypeScript. Looks like a priority list: the first rule whose name matches wins.
const const_access_rules: Record<string, string> = JSON.parse('{"staff": "allow-ward", "visitor": "deny"}')
const_access_rules["0"] = "allow-all"  // a rule added later, perhaps from user-supplied JSON
console.log(Object.keys(const_access_rules)[0]) // 0: the newest rule is now the first one checked
```

```ts
// The check that holds: the priority is data, not key order.
const const_ranked_rules: { rule_name: string; rule_action: string; rule_priority: number }[] = [
    { rule_name: "staff", rule_action: "allow-ward", rule_priority: 1 },
    { rule_name: "visitor", rule_action: "deny", rule_priority: 2 },
    { rule_name: "0", rule_action: "allow-all", rule_priority: 99 },
]
console.log(const_ranked_rules.toSorted((left_rule, right_rule) => left_rule.rule_priority - right_rule.rule_priority)[0].rule_name) // staff
```

The class is [CWE-696](https://cwe.mitre.org/data/definitions/696.html), "Incorrect Behavior Order": "The product performs multiple related behaviors, but the behaviors are performed in the wrong order in ways that may produce resultant weaknesses." The baseline is Rust, whose default map order cannot be relied on at all, so a priority has to be written down.

| Language | What goes wrong | What the language does | Still on you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | nothing reliable to misuse in `HashMap` order | randomizes it per run; `BTreeMap` orders by key | a first-match list is a `Vec`, sorted by a stated priority |
| C++ | a pointer-keyed set orders by allocation history | `std::less` gives a total order, not a meaningful one | key containers by a value, never by address, when order matters |
| F# | `Dictionary` order follows edit history | nothing; the docs say "undefined" | use a list with an explicit priority, or `Map` with a priority key |
| TypeScript | an index-like key jumps to the front | nothing at the type level | keep priorities as data; never let key order decide |

Which of your "first match wins" rules iterate a map or an object, and who can add a key to it?

# FAQ

**Can sorting by hospital alone really score 0.86?** Yes, and the arithmetic shows why. A scorer that gives every Mount Sinai X-ray 0.342 and every NIH X-ray 0.012 wins every pair of a Mount Sinai pneumonia against an NIH healthy case and ties within each hospital. With the paper's prevalences and, as an assumption, the hospital-ID test-set sizes (8,388 Mount Sinai and 22,062 NIH; the paper does not give the pneumonia test-set sizes in the text), it scores 0.857, close to the paper's 0.861:

```rust
// Rust 1.99.1. Rates from the paper; test-set sizes assumed (see above).
fn main() {
    let msh_test_count: f64 = 8_388.0;
    let nih_test_count: f64 = 22_062.0;
    let msh_positive_count = (msh_test_count * 0.342).round();
    let nih_positive_count = (nih_test_count * 0.012).round();
    let msh_negative_count = msh_test_count - msh_positive_count;
    let nih_negative_count = nih_test_count - nih_positive_count;
    let wins = msh_positive_count * nih_negative_count; // an MSH positive outscores an NIH negative
    let ties = msh_positive_count * msh_negative_count + nih_positive_count * nih_negative_count;
    let pairs = (msh_positive_count + nih_positive_count) * (msh_negative_count + nih_negative_count);
    println!("AUC from the hospital alone: {:.3}", (wins + 0.5 * ties) / pairs); // 0.857
}
```

**Did the paper find a bug in the network?** No. The authors are clear that the network used real signal in the images; the problem was that the signal included where the image was taken, and "CNNs could rely on subtle differences in acquisition protocol, image processing, or distribution pipeline (e.g., image compression) and overlook pathology."

**Is "the AI learned the wall clock" a real incident?** Not that this special could find. See the end of Appendix A.

# Appendix A: the Sendai subway, where every rule was written down

Not part of the tip rotation. Written 2026-11-03 as the contrast to this part's story: a "smart" controller from the 1980s whose knowledge a person could read. A real system, from Hitachi's own reports, two of them in Japanese.

**Bug class:** not a bug: the contrast. The fuzzy controller did what its designers wrote, and they could say why.

**The story, in plain words:**

On 15 July 1987 Sendai, the "city of trees", became the ninth city in Japan with a subway: 13.59 km, 16 stations, 19 trains, about 200,000 riders a day, according to Hitachi's technical journal. Its trains drove themselves from one station to the next, and the controller doing it used fuzzy logic, rules that work with degrees of truth such as "the ride is fairly comfortable" instead of only yes or no. Hitachi called its "predictive fuzzy control" a world first.

It began with one engineer reading one article. Seiji Yasunobu, at Hitachi's Systems Development Laboratory since 1975, read Michio Sugeno's 1979 article on applying fuzzy sets and logic to control, and from April 1979 began rewriting an automatic train controller that used conventional PID control (a formula that steers by the error, its sum and its rate of change). His goal, in his own words, was automatic driving "equal to a skilled driver": short running times, a comfortable ride, an accurate stop, whatever the passenger load, the gradient or the train.

So the rules came from people. The controller predicted what each possible brake or power command would do to the ride and the stop, scored those outcomes the way a skilled driver would judge them, every 0.1 seconds, and picked the best. Yasunobu even worried about the name: "aimai" (vague) control sounded wrong for something that carries passengers, and lowercase "fuzzy" sounded flimsy, so he wrote it as "Fuzzy", capitalised.

Then came the hard part. The microcomputers were 8-bit, with almost no development tools, and the program had to handle distances near 1,000 m and speeds near 100 km/h while stopping to the centimetre. Even "double precision" on that machine was a 16-bit integer, at most 65,535, and the program squared speeds often, so every calculation had to dodge overflow (the same trap as part 2's Ariane 5, solved instead of shipped). The first version, he admits, was "ii-kagen seigyo", sloppy control: no one knew what it would do on the real hardware. After every step was tuned, it became "accurate, yoi-kagen" control, a pun on "just right".

The research formally ended in March 1983, and the work was handed to Hitachi's development department to be built as a product for the Sendai subway. That July he still got to try it on a real train, "late at night after revenue service ended", and came away convinced it would work. When the line opened four years later, he wrote, it was "unexpected" to him how much attention it drew. Hitachi's 1988 report says the result improved ride comfort, stopping accuracy and energy saving; neither source gives the numbers.

The contrast with this part's story is the whole point. Sendai's controller was "smart" in the 1980s sense, but every rule in it was something a person had written down from a skilled driver's judgement and could read back. The Zech network's real rule, "this looks like Mount Sinai", was written down by nobody, and its authors found it only by examining the images by hand.

## A.2 The story this part started from

The idea for this bonus part was a story often told about Japan: an AI watching a station platform learned to close the train doors by reading the wall clock, so it waited on weekends when trains ran early. This special could not find it in any report. The closest telling is an anonymous forum post about turnstiles, not doors, and it matches a family of "the AI learned the wrong thing" stories that Gwern Branwen's investigation of the best-known one, the tank detector that learned the weather, concludes "is definitely not real as usually told: it is just an urban legend". Sendai's real fuzzy controller drove the trains; nothing in Hitachi's reports has it watching doors or a clock. The Zech study is the documented version of the idea.
